# xmb-waves: functional specification (strict clean-room tier)

This document describes an effect: a flowing, luminous wave drifting across a softly coloured background, with glowing sparkles around it, whose colours follow the time of day and the month. It is the kind of animated menu background a game console might show.

It describes **what the viewer should see, how it should behave, and how the original is built in general terms**. Construction is given as recommended design: "the original does X; do the same, with your own values". It contains no colours, palettes, textures, constants, preset values or code from the original. The only numbers taken from the analysis of the original are the few listed in section 11 ("Structural numbers from observation and analysis"). Every other value, colour, texture and implementation detail is **your design**. Tune it by comparing your renders side by side with genuine public footage of the original (section 10). Where this document says "choose", the choice is yours.

The current version is 31; the version history is at the end of this document.

## 1. Output and platform

- A single static web page, `index.html`, rendering with WebGL2. No build step is required (a build step is fine if the output is a static page). No network requests at run time; every texture is generated in code or shipped as your own original asset.
- The picture has a 16:9 aspect by default and must render correctly at any resolution, from small previews to 4K and above. 4:3 is a nice-to-have: show the same scene without stretching it, only narrower at the sides.
- It keeps animating indefinitely without visible loops or drift. In normal use it follows the viewer's local clock and date.
- **Performance target:** smooth 60 frames per second on a recent iPad in Safari **at render scale 4x** (section 8). Design for it:
  - Run the simulation (wave and sparkles) off the main thread in a **Web Worker**, on its own fixed-step clock, and hand finished vertex data to the main thread without copying (transferable buffers). The main thread only draws the newest finished state.
  - Keep the GPU passes lean: few full-screen passes, small intermediate targets wherever the content is soft (background, glare), no needless read-backs from the GPU, no per-draw error queries.
  - When the render is larger than the picture on screen, **average it down to the screen's pixel size** in your own final pass. Do not hand the browser a canvas at the full render size to scale.
  - Cap the frame rate at 60 frames per second on high-refresh displays.

## 2. Composition

The picture is built from these layers, back to front:

1. **Background**: a smooth colour gradient filling the screen.
2. **Wave**: a translucent, glowing sheet that crosses the screen roughly horizontally.
3. **Glare**: a soft bloom around the brightest parts of the background and wave.
4. **Sparkles**: many tiny glowing particles floating around the wave, plus a fainter glint on each.
5. **Dither**: a very faint per-pixel noise that hides colour banding in the smooth gradients.

All the luminous layers (wave, glare, sparkles) add light to what is behind them (additive blending); nothing in the effect darkens or hides the background. The result is clamped to the displayable range.

**Order of operations in the original** (recommended):

1. Compute this frame's low-resolution background gradient (section 3).
2. Draw the wave into its own off-screen intensity buffer (section 4.5).
3. Composite: background plus wave light, through the mood's exposure and tone curve (4.5), into the screen image.
4. Build the glare from that composite and add it (section 5).
5. Draw the sparkles additively on top, then their glint pass (section 6).
6. Add the dither just before the image is written out at 8 bits per channel.

**Dither (construction).** The original adds fresh per-pixel noise every frame: independent from pixel to pixel and from frame to frame, uniformly distributed, the same value on all three channels, scaled so that it never moves a pixel by more than about one output level. It is added before the final 8-bit rounding. Any uniform or ordered dither with the same properties is fine.

## 3. Background

### 3.1 Look

- A smooth gradient, mostly vertical (brightness and hue change from top to bottom), with gentle, sometimes curved or radial variation across the width. No visible bands or edges. **No compact bright shapes (version 19):** the lights described under Construction are broad, so each reads as a part of the picture being lifted rather than an object sitting in it. At every hour, a gently curved surface fits any part of the picture closely, with very little left over.
- **The structure is not only vertical (version 23).** At any fixed height the colour changes across the width, and it changes differently at different heights: the left and the right of the picture can differ low down while agreeing at the top, or differ the other way round, and a part of the picture can be warmer or cooler than its surroundings without being any brighter. Some of that moves over the day and some of it does not. What stays where it is from hour to hour, keeping the same shape all day long in one month, belongs to that month's own images rather than to the lighting; comparing the same month at hours well apart separates the two, and that split is worth making before anything is fitted.
- Its palette depends on the **month**: each month has its own palette (warm, cool, green, pink and so on). For example October's is a warm gold/brown. Choose twelve palettes that are pleasant and clearly distinct, and tune them against the public footage. **Every month wants footage of its own month (version 33).** A palette tuned on the months a measurement set happens to cover is tuned blind in the months it does not, and those months come out plainly the wrong colour - all day, over whole regions of the frame - in a way no exposure, no tone setting and no later fit of the lighting repairs, because what is wrong is the picture rather than the light on it. A set that covers only some of the months cannot see any of this, so a build can read as settled while most of its year is wrong. So get conditions in every month into the set before the palettes are fitted, and report how close a build is month by month rather than only over the set as a whole. **And footage of a day on which that month shows alone (version 34).** Footage of a month is not enough on its own if all of it falls around the middle of the month. There two palettes are mixed over the whole frame and the mid-month lowering of the saturation is at its deepest, so what such a set pins is the **mixture** of a month and its neighbour and not either palette: two palettes can both be plainly wrong - the wrong brightness and the wrong hue, over whole regions, wrong to the eye rather than to a measurement - while their mixture on that day is right, and nothing in such a set will say so. So every month wants at least one condition near the start of a month, before next month's picture has appeared and while that month's own palette is all there is to see. What shows whether a build has this fault: take one month at a day it shows alone and at a day in the middle of the month, and compare how the picture changes between those two days with how the original's changes between them. A build whose own change runs the other way from the original's has that month's picture wrong, and its agreement in the middle of the month is the mixture covering for it.
- It depends on the **time of day**: the same month looks brighter around midday and much darker at night, with dawn and dusk in between. Through the daytime its lighting also shifts: the brighter part of the background travels from one side to the other over the course of the day (version 17: see "The light" under Construction).
- **At night (version 17; corrects "a faint hint").** The upper part of the screen goes black, but the lower part keeps a clear, dim glow in the month's own colour. It is a recognisable low glow, not a near-black screen with a trace of colour. **(Version 19.)** That glow is a broad, even rise towards the bottom edge, not a patch sitting somewhere in the frame; its brighter side lies towards one side edge early in the night and has travelled to the other by morning. **(Version 23.)** Its lean to one side is not all the travelling light. Part of the difference between the left and the right of the lower part of the picture is the same at every hour of the night, changing only as slowly as the month's own colours do, and that part belongs to the month's night picture rather than to its lighting; how large it is, and which way it leans, differ from month to month. So the lower part's left-to-right balance at night, and the colour of its lower corners, are a reading of the night image as much as of the night light, and they are read from footage month by month. **Which part is which, read over the night (version 33).** The painted part cannot change within one night, and the lit part travels across it. So what separates them is not the lean at one hour but the way that lean changes between hours of the same night. Read the lower part's left-to-right balance at two or three hours of one night: in the original it runs steadily one way through the night, following the light's own travel across the width, and a painted lean can move all of those readings together but can never reverse their order. A build whose balance changes the other way through the night has no travelling night light in its lower picture at all, however well its lean at any single hour agrees. The same goes for the gentle arch along the very bottom of the frame: in the original there is one at every hour of the night, and it moves across the width, where an arch painted into the picture can only be right at one hour and sits still at the rest.
- **Over the day (version 17).** The background stays dark well into the small hours. It is darkest a couple of hours after midnight, darker even than at midnight itself, then brightens steeply to near its daytime level by early morning and keeps brightening gently into the afternoon. In the evening it fades over a few hours (hours in section 11, N8). It does not brighten as soon as the night weight starts to fade.
- **The day's own light holds up into the evening (version 28).** Set the night weight aside and look only at the light the day picture is seen by. It does not rise and fall evenly about midday. It comes up the gradual way through the small hours and the early morning; it is at nearly its full height from the middle of the morning right through to the early evening, so little changed across the whole of that stretch that what makes the picture at midday differ from the picture in the early evening is where the lights have moved to and the composite's own exposure rather than the day's level; and then it falls away quickly, late in the evening. Its lowest point is local midnight. (The hours in section 11, N8, are the **picture's** brightness, which is a different thing: it carries the night weight as well.) A build whose day level peaks at midday and falls away as gradually as it rose is both too bright around midday and too dark in the early evening at once, and nothing applied afterwards repairs it: one exposure for each mood and one palette for each month cannot lift the early evening without lifting midday with it.
- **The top of the picture over the day (version 19; replaces version 17's "Dawn and dusk").** The top follows the light and nothing else. Around midday, with the light at its highest, the top is about as light as the rest of the picture. As the light drops towards either end of the day the top loses its light altogether and darkens, and through the night it is black. Plotted against the hour, the top's brightness relative to the bottom's traces one smooth arc over the day with a single maximum: there is no extra darkening attached to particular hours of the clock, and no second dip. Dawn and dusk are in no way special to the top; all that happens there is that the light has moved away from it.
- **Three things happen in the evening, and not together (version 28).** As the evening wears on, the top of the picture loses the day's light and goes dark; the picture's own level falls away; and the colour low in the frame deepens towards its purest. They do not arrive at once. The top goes first, and most gradually. The level's fall and the deepening of the colour come later and faster, and they come together. In the small hours the three unwind in the other order: the colour comes out of its deepest first and fastest, then the level rises and the top comes back, both gradually. So there are hours in the evening at which the top has gone while the lower picture is still near its daytime level, and hours in the small hours at which the lower picture has come back while the colour low down is still deep. Two curves against the hour show all of it - the grey of a band near the top of the frame and the grey of a band near the bottom - and the pace of each is what there is to fit (see Construction).
- **The deepest colours come late (version 19).** The purest, most saturated colours of the whole day are not at dawn or at dusk but late in the evening and through the small hours, and they are low in the frame, where part of the day picture still shows under the night; there the colour can go as far as a fully pure hue. Through the daytime, by contrast, the saturation at a given height barely changes with the hour, apart from the paling where a light passes (see Construction).
- **Mid-month (version 17; version 19: not slight).** The colours are less saturated around the middle of the month, while two months' palettes are mixed, than at its start or its end, and plainly so rather than only just. **(Version 34: how far through the month it reaches, and what pins its breadth.)** The lowering is not confined to the middle of the month. It is plainly there several days either side of the middle, it dies away only gradually towards the month's ends, and even on a month's first and last days it has not come back to nothing: a trace of it survives the whole month through. A build that is back to full saturation at the month's ends has it too narrow, and the days at which the two part company most are the days no usual set holds. What pins the breadth: the mean saturation of a band along the very bottom of the frame - the band no wave ever covers - at one fixed daytime hour, read through the days of one month with that month's own level divided out, and compared between days about a quarter and about three quarters of the way through the month, the middle day, and the first and last days. What cannot pin it: the breadth and the depth are two numbers, and a day in the middle of a month together with a day at its end give only one reading between them. A build fitted on those two days alone can sit exactly on the original at both of them with its breadth and its depth wrong in compensating directions, and be plainly wrong on every day between them; the days that break that tie lie in the first and the last third of a month.
- Changes are always gradual: no flicker from frame to frame and no visible jumps as the clock advances.

### 3.2 Construction (how the original does it; do the same with your own palettes)

- **Two small images per month (version 23: what is in them).** Each month has a *day* image and a *night* image: small, low-resolution colour images. They are painted pictures rather than shapes computed from a handful of parameters, and all of the background's own structure - everything the lighting does not put there - is in them. Their colour varies smoothly in every direction: down the image, across it and diagonally. The hue drifts along the image independently of its brightness, so two parts of one image are two different colours rather than one colour at two brightnesses. And the side-to-side variation low down need not match the one higher up, in direction as well as in size: in some months the left is the warmer or brighter side at the bottom of the image and the cooler or dimmer one at the top. The night image is a dark version of the month's look, with structure of its own of the same kind, not a flat wash.
  - **Give your images enough freedom to hold that (version 23).** A vertical ramp with a soft blob on it and a side-to-side tilt cannot. One tilt that does not change with height cannot say that the left-to-right difference reverses down the frame; a ramp and a blob cannot let the hue wander where the brightness does not; and in some months there is more going on than one broad feature can carry. Whatever such a family cannot express is exactly what is left over once a build of that kind has been fitted as well as it possibly can be, and it shows as a steady error in particular parts of the frame that no choice of lights, palettes or tone settings removes: the lights move over the day and this does not, so there is nothing for them to cancel it with. Describe each image in a way that can hold any smooth picture - for instance a small grid of colours you choose freely, read with smooth interpolation - and fit those colours against footage, rather than adding lighting terms to make up for what the image cannot say.
    - **Two shapes in particular (version 33).** Both are shapes a description can fail to hold while looking perfectly reasonable, and a build that cannot hold them pays for it at every hour of the day. **First, a bend across the width that is not at the middle.** In some months the darkest place across the width lies between the middle and one edge; in others the steepest part of the left-to-right change does. A description that can only tilt, or only arch about the middle of the width, cannot put the bend where the picture has it, and what it leaves behind is a pair of neighbouring places low in the frame wrong in opposite directions - one too warm, the other too cool, in the same spot at every hour - which in the months whose pictures are least like a plain tilt is the larger part of the hour-independent error such a build carries. **Second, the fall up the frame just above the bottom edge.** The picture stays nearly level for a short way above the edge and then gives way; holding that knee needs the places the picture is described at to crowd towards the bottom edge rather than to be spread evenly up it. The two are independent: freedom across the width buys nothing for the knee, and places crowded low down buy nothing for the width. And a month whose picture happens to be a plain tilt is held well by almost any description, so do not take one month's close agreement as evidence that a description is adequate - it may only be the easiest month to paint.
  - **And no more freedom than that (version 23; version 33: what the restraint is, and what it is not).** Keep the description as coarse as will do. The stored pictures are smooth: no edge or band appears anywhere in the frame at any hour, and a description fine enough to show one is already too fine. A fine grid can also take in the footage's own noise, and worse, it can soak up an error that really belongs to the lighting, because not everything the lighting does moves over the day either: the share of the image that the lights never touch does not move with the hour at all. So prefer the coarsest description that leaves no visible large-scale difference from the original anywhere in the frame, region by region, and fit the lighting terms named below at the same time rather than afterwards. **(Version 33.)** Three things make that a usable test rather than a reason to stay coarse.
    - **The test is what the description can express, not how smooth the result is.** Described more coarsely, a picture only comes out smoother - a coarse description cannot make an edge or a band that the picture it approximates does not already have - so how far a build's own gradient departs from a smooth surface says nothing at all about whether its description is too fine, and refining in either of the two directions above cannot put an edge in the frame. Judge the description by whether it can hold the shapes above, and refine until it can.
    - **Which of the two faults you have can be read from footage you already have.** Split the difference from the original into the part that stands still over the hours of one month and the part that moves with them. A description too coarse to hold the picture leaves a residue that stands still, largest in the months whose pictures are least like a plain tilt and shaped like neighbouring places wrong in opposite directions in one fixed spot; a poorly fitted light leaves a residue that travels across the frame with the day. Where the standing part is the larger, refine the description; where the moving part is, look to the lights and to the curves of the clock.
    - **Do not refine the picture to chase something the lighting does.** The arch along the bottom edge at night is the travelling night light's doing, not the picture's: across their width the painted night pictures lean or ramp one way, with no hump standing inside the frame (INFERRED for the months no footage has been read in), so the painted lean of 3.1 is still there and still to be read - what cannot be painted is an arch, and still less one that moves. A description refined, or an image re-fitted, while that light is not reaching the lower picture will paint the light's own shape into the picture, where it cannot travel: the result is exact at the one hour it was fitted at and wrong at every other hour of the night. So settle the night light first (see **The night images** below), and the picture after it.
- **The day images are processed, not shown as they are (version 19; replaces version 17's "The images are processed").** Every frame the original converts each day image's colours to hue, saturation and value, scales the value and the saturation by the lighting terms below, and converts back. Lit areas become brighter and clearly paler (less saturated): the paling is one of the ways the picture's structure shows, not a small correction. Around the middle of the month the saturation is lowered, and not slightly. The night images are not treated this way at all: see **The night images** below.
- **Each month's image is processed on its own, and the processed results are then mixed (version 19).** The order of the two steps matters, because converting to hue, saturation and value and scaling the saturation is not a linear operation. Mixing the two months' colours first and putting the single mixed colour through the processing once gives a different picture: around the middle of the month the bands come out more saturated and darker than they should. That error is nothing at the start and at the end of a month, largest in the middle, and the part of the frame it spoils climbs from the bottom towards the top as the month passes, following the step described under **Within the month**; it leaves the hue almost untouched, so hue is the wrong thing to check it by. Because it disappears at both ends of the month and moves about the frame within it, no choice of palette can stand in for doing the two steps in the right order. It is also invisible wherever two consecutive palettes happen to share a hue, so a build that mixes first can look right for some months and be wrong for others.
- **Within the month (version 17; corrects earlier versions).** The change to next month's images is not a uniform blend over the whole screen: next month's colours **rise from the bottom of the screen**. Down the screen the mix follows a soft S-shaped step from one month to the other, sharp early and late in the month and very broad in the middle. For about the first week this month's images show alone. Then next month's appear at the bottom and climb. Around the middle of the month the two are mixed about half and half over the whole screen for a few days. Then next month's takes over the bottom first and the top last, and over the last few days of the month next month's images show alone. The month's progress is counted in whole days (section 11, N7). December's next month is January.
  - **Fit the step; the words above do not pin it (version 34).** Where that step sits and how broad it is, day by day, are to be fitted from footage like anything else. A build can satisfy every sentence above and still have next month's picture well above or well below the original's place in the frame on the days between, which is worth more on screen than any other difference in the middle of the month. The two readings that pin it, at one fixed daytime hour and through the days of one month: the height of the row at which the picture is halfway between the colours that same month shows on its own first day and on its last, and the height over which that halfway crossing is spread. Read them from the bottom edge upwards, and check which end of your own reading is which against a night condition, where the picture is bright along the bottom and black at the top: an inverted reading turns this observable's answer upside down and makes a build that is wrong low in the frame look wrong high up. The days to read are the ones a usual set never holds, because this difference goes to nothing at the middle of the month and at both its ends and is at its largest about a third and about two thirds of the way through: a set made of a mid-month day and an end-of-month day cannot see it at all.
- **Day and night (version 17; corrects earlier versions. Version 19: the daylight factor and the shaping of the day image down the frame).** A night weight follows the local hour. It fades in smoothly (ease-in-out) in the evening, fades out smoothly through the small hours, and is zero through the daytime (hours in section 11, N6). This schedule is the background's own, separate from the four moods in section 7. Things that are easy to miss:
  - Even at full night weight the picture is **a mix** of the night image and the day image, never the night image alone: the day image keeps a large share.
  - The day image has its own **daylight** factor, built from the warped hour below and from nothing else: a smooth rise through the warped morning and a smooth fall through the warped evening. Because it shares the warp with the lights, the factor's pace and the lights' pace are tied: the factor cannot be fitted with the warp held at an arbitrary value, nor the warp without the factor. **(Version 23 corrects the rest of what version 19 said here.** Version 19 went on to say that how steep the morning rise is and how slowly the evening fades are consequences of that warp, so that they are not free choices and cannot be set independently of each other. That half is withdrawn: the shared warp stands, the two slopes do not follow from it, and the next bullet says what they are instead.**)**
  - **One pair of steps, two rates, and all the curves they make (version 28; replaces version 23's "That factor is not symmetric about midday" and keeps its finding that the factor is asymmetric).** Every curve of the clock that the background needs is built from **one pair** of smooth steps: one placed in the warped morning, one in the warped evening, either side of the warped midday. Three things about that pair carry the whole of this.
    - **Where the pair sits** is settled by the lights rather than fitted on its own: the two places are the ones at which the travelling spot's centre crosses the bottom edge of the picture (see **How far the spot rises and falls** below), so the oval and these curves turn at the same two moments of the warped hour.
    - **The shape of a step.** Each one leaves one value and approaches the other, closely but never exactly, and it goes on approaching for as long as the clock runs; it has no stretch of the clock outside which it is flat. A step that reaches its end value over a finite stretch, with a floor added back afterwards to stand in for the missing tail, is a different shape - flat where the original is still changing - and it will not fit at any setting. This is the half of this bullet that matters most: none of the rest of it helps unless the step is of this kind.
    - **Two rates, and only two.** Each of the pair exists at a gentler and a sharper rate, and the two are in a fixed proportion to each other which does not change with the hour or the month. So what there is to fit here is one rate and that one proportion - two rates in all, for the whole build, not a width for each end of each curve.
    Every curve is then a product of a morning step and an evening step, and which rate goes where is what distinguishes them. The day image has three:
    - the **daylight** factor above is the **gentler** morning step times the **sharper** evening step. That is where its asymmetry comes from, and it is the whole of it: the factor rises the gradual way, holds near its full height from the middle of the morning until well into the evening, and then falls the fast way, so its highest point lies in the later part of the afternoon rather than at noon.
    - the clock of the **value** shaping down the frame (below) is the **gentler** step at **both** ends. It is therefore symmetric about midday, and the stretch of the clock over which that shaping acts is the widest of the three: the top of the picture begins to lose the day image earliest in the evening and gets it back latest in the morning.
    - the clock of the **saturation** shaping down the frame (below) is the **sharper** step at **both** ends. It too is symmetric about midday, and the stretch over which that shaping acts is the narrowest of the three: the colour low in the frame deepens last in the evening and comes out of it first in the morning.
    The night image's two curves - its envelope across the night and its own shaping down the frame, both below - are two of these same products read on its own unwarped clock, so one pair of steps serves the whole of the background.
    (This restores part of what version 19 claimed and version 23 withdrew. The two rates are not free of each other after all; what version 19 had wrong was the reason, since they do not follow from the warp, and what version 23 had right is that the evening must be free to differ from the morning. One pair of steps at two rates in fixed proportion satisfies both.)
    Three warnings, each of which has already been paid for by a build. **Do not give the three curves a free rate each.** That is more freedom than the original has, and a fit pinned at a handful of hours of the working day will put the asymmetry on the shaping curve and leave the daylight factor symmetric: that agrees with the original at those hours and parts company from it between them, and then no exposure and no palette can repair it, because the error at midday and the error in the early evening want opposite corrections. **Do not let the fit choose which end of the day is the sharp one.** The mirror image of the arrangement above - sharper in the morning, gentler in the evening - fits the hours around midday, dusk and midnight better than the measurement floor and is then badly wrong in the small hours, so it has to be taken from this text rather than fitted. **Do not fit these curves after the lights, the images and the palettes:** they share the warp with the lights and their level with everything else, so they belong in the same fit.
    What pins them, with no new footage: the picture's own level at midday against its level in the early evening of the same month (the daylight factor is at nearly its full height at both, so a build whose own level falls away between them has the asymmetry wrong, however it scales the result afterwards); the night conditions, which see the same two rates through the night image's envelope; and the two band curves of 3.1 wherever the footage reaches into the evening. What pins them better: one condition inside the morning rise, where nothing in the usual set sits. Of all the hours between the usual ones, that is the one worth asking for.
  - **That factor has no dip of its own (version 19).** It is at its lowest at local midnight, where it is close to nothing, and rises from there without turning back. The hours in N8 describe the brightness of the **picture**, which is a different thing. The picture's minimum a couple of hours after midnight is a result of the mix: the night weight is fading out while the daylight factor is still low, so the mix shifts towards a day image that is still dark, and the picture brightens only as daylight rises. Putting a local minimum into the day image's own level instead reproduces the brightness curve but gets the colour and the vertical shape of the small hours wrong, and leaves a share of the day image at midnight that should not be there. **(Version 28.)** Close to nothing is not nothing: because of the shape of the step, a share of the day image survives the whole of the night, and there is no stretch of the clock over which it is exactly gone. What shows that share is colour rather than brightness - the hue and the saturation of the lower part of the picture through the hours around midnight - because brightness there is shared with the night image's own level and its envelope and colour is not.
  - **The day image is also shaped down the frame, by the clock alone (version 19; version 28: how it varies with the height, and the saturation's own clock).** On top of the daylight factor, the day image's value is reduced towards the top of the frame, by an amount that grows with height up the frame and whose strength is itself a function of the warped hour: nothing at all from early morning until late evening, and strong through the late evening and the small hours. So late in the evening and through the small hours the day image has gone at the top of the frame â€” the top is fully black there, not merely dim â€” while a clear share of it survives low down and keeps a day-like colour in the lower part of the night picture. Both shapings depend on the hour only, never on where the lights are or on the width of the frame, and both are switched off through the whole working day, so neither is a dawn or dusk effect; at local midnight itself they have little to act on, the day image's level already being as low as it goes.
    - **How it varies with the height (version 28).** The reduction is **proportional**: every further step up the frame takes away the same share of what is left there, so what survives falls off geometrically with height, and the bottom row of the picture is not touched at all, at any hour. Read up the frame on a logarithmic scale it is a straight line, whose slope is the clock's. Two things follow. The day image approaches nothing towards the top without ever reaching it, so there is no height at which it stops; a law that subtracts and then has to be held at zero does stop at some height, and that height travels up and down the frame as the clock's strength changes. (On a coarse buffer read through a smooth upscale that leaves no edge a viewer could find, so it is a fault waiting to happen rather than one that shows - it would show if the background were ever computed at the screen's own resolution. Such a law is also at its steepest at the very bottom edge, where the original's is at its gentlest.) What matters more is that the two laws differ most in the **middle** of the frame and all the way up it: a proportional law takes its share everywhere, while a law that subtracts a concave power of the height barely bites low down and then falls off a cliff, so it leaves far too much of the day image high in the frame in the shoulder hours. What pins it, at a single hour: the share of the bottom row's brightness that survives at each height up the frame, read at one hour in the shoulder of the evening or of the small hours. There the two laws are far apart; they agree only in the middle of the night, where the day image has already gone. So which hour is read matters more than how many.
    - **One rate in the height, and three uses of it (version 33).** That rate is not this shaping's alone. One and the same rate in the height governs every fall of this kind in the background: this shaping of the day image's value, the raise of the day image's saturation below, and the shaping of the night image's level up the frame. All three fall off at that one rate, and what distinguishes them is only which clock drives each - and, for the saturation, that the fall is read the other way up, as a rise which levels off towards a ceiling near the top of the frame rather than growing without limit (see below). So there is one rate here to be fitted for the whole background, not three. Which of the three it is fitted on matters: the footage pins it sharply through the day image's value and barely at all through the other two, so fit it where it is pinned and let the other two follow it. A build that gives each of the three a rate of its own can sit inside the footage's own spread at every condition a usual set covers and still be well out at hours between them, in the colour of the picture as much as in its brightness - and the two it cannot pin are then free to drift to values at which their mechanisms do nothing at all.
    - **The saturation's shaping has its own clock (version 28; corrects version 19's "shaped in the same way and at the same hours"; version 33: and what its law is).** The saturation is shaped by the same law in the height at the same rate (above), read the other way up: the saturation's raise above its bottom-row level is what the value shaping's fall takes away, read as a rise - smallest at the bottom edge, growing as the fall dies away, and levelling off towards the top of the frame, approaching a ceiling it never passes. It does not grow without limit up the frame, and the ceiling is a level to be read from footage, by the ratio named below. But it is driven by the sharp-at-both-ends curve above, not by the one that drives the value shaping. So the two do not turn together: there are hours in the evening at which the top has already lost the day image while the colour low in the frame has not begun to deepen, and the reverse on the way out. A build that drives both from one curve cannot show that, and because the one curve has to serve both, it is the top of the frame that pays: too much of the day image is left there in the early evening. The raising is not held back at the pure end either: the parts of the day image furthest up the frame are carried past a pure hue of the image's own colour, and rather than stopping there the colour swings towards whichever of the three primaries is strongest in it, so the hue itself turns as the shaping deepens.
      - **Where this can be read at all, and the trap beside it (version 33).** The saturation shaping shows only where its clock is open **and** the day image still has light to colour: one narrow stretch of the late evening, another in the small hours, and nowhere else - through the working day it does nothing. Nor can it be read at the top of the frame, because the composite crushes the dark top of the picture until its saturation reads fully pure there whatever the picture says. So read it at and below the middle of the frame, and read it as a ratio: the saturation of a band across the middle of the frame against the saturation of a band at the bottom, taken from the background with the wave's own pixels left out, since in a whole frame the wave pales the middle band and the reading is then not the picture's. That ratio is also what keeps a fit honest while the proportion between the two rates is being settled, and it is worth taking before anything here is changed: a build can have the raise's own strength and that proportion wrong in opposite directions, agree with the original in every brightness reading it takes, and then move this ratio further the wrong way when the proportion alone is corrected. Read it at one hour of the late-evening shoulder and at one in the small hours.
- **The night images (version 19; replaces version 17's "The night image").** The night pair is mixed for the month and then scaled in brightness only. None of the colour processing above touches it: no paling where it is lit, no deepening of its colour towards the top of the frame, no mid-month lowering (its brightness is another matter - see the last bullet below). The night glow therefore always shows its own palette's hue and saturation, in its lit parts as much as its unlit ones and whatever the day of the month. **(Version 23.)** The night image's own painted structure matters as much as the day image's, and in the same way: the glow's side-to-side lean low in the frame, and the colour of its lower corners, are partly painted in rather than all lighting, and the painted part stays where it is while the night light travels across the night. Give the night image the same freedom, and the same restraint, as the day image above. One colour times a vertical shape cannot hold this, and a build of that kind carries a steady error low down at every night condition that no strength of light and no choice of shape will remove, because the lighting here can only brighten and dim what the image already says.
  - **How high the glow climbs is the image's doing, not the light's (version 28).** How far up the frame the night glow reaches, and how quickly it gives out, is almost all the night image's own fall up the picture rather than the reach of the light along the bottom edge: changing how tall that light is, where its centre sits, how strong either light is, or how low the level is allowed to go moves the height the glow reaches very little, while the painted fall moves it as far as you please. So read it from footage as a property of the image: at a fixed share of the frame's height above the bottom edge, how much of the bottom band's brightness is left, and at what height it has halved, month by month at the dead of night. Then give the night image enough rows up the frame to hold the answer, because a description too coarse up the frame will pull that fall towards the average of the months it is fitted on, which is a large part of the error a build of that kind shows here. And keep in mind that only the months the footage covers can be read this way at all.
  - Its brightness follows the same two lights as the day image, but driven by a clock of its own that is **not** warped: it turns once a day at a steady rate, counted from midday. Over the night its light travels the whole of its path: it rises at the left edge low down in the evening, is high in the middle at midnight, sets at the right edge low down towards morning, and is below the picture through the day. A clock that covers only part of a turn, or that is warped like the day's, is wrong: this light has to cross the frame over the night, smoothly and with no jump at midnight.
  - Its level also follows a **time envelope across the night**, lowest at dusk and at dawn and highest in the middle of the night, and the result is held above a fixed floor from below, so it never falls below a sizeable fraction of its full level. Because the floor applies after the lighting, near dusk and near dawn the night image is at its floor almost everywhere: it shows as a flat, even wash of the month's colour with none of the light's structure in it. Only around the middle of the night does it rise clear of the floor, and only then does the travelling light show through it. **(Version 28.)** That envelope is one of the products described above, read on this unwarped clock: the gentler step as the evening comes on times the sharper one at dawn. So it is not symmetric about the middle of the night either - it comes up gradually through the evening and gives way quickly at dawn. The night image's level is also shaped down the frame by the same proportional law as the day image's, driven by the gentler step at both ends of the same unwarped clock, so it takes almost nothing out at the dead of night and a great deal at either end of the night. That is a small effect at the hours of the night the usual footage covers, but it is a mechanism rather than a number, and a build without it paints its absence into the night image instead - where it does not belong, because one painted fall has to serve every hour of the night at once.
    - **The floor comes last (version 33).** It is applied to the whole of the night image's level: after the envelope, after the shaping up the frame and after the lighting. So wherever it binds, the night picture is flat from the bottom edge to the top - neither the light's structure nor the shaping's fall is in it - and the shaping cannot act below it. A build that holds the level up before shaping it keeps falling away up the frame where the original is flat, at exactly the hours where the floor binds and the shaping's clock is strong at once, which is the shoulder of the evening and of the morning.
    - **The floor is for the ends of the night, not the middle of it (version 33).** Around the middle of the night the lit level stands clear of the floor everywhere the light reaches, so the travelling glow does show along the bottom of the picture: the bottom edge arches gently under it, and that arch moves across the width as the night passes. If a build's bottom edge instead keeps one shape all night and differs between the hours only in level, then its floor is standing above its lit level there, its travelling light is contributing nothing at all, and no reading taken at one hour of the night will say so. Worse, its fit will then put the light's work into the painted picture, where it cannot travel. The reading that settles it is the one in 3.1: the direction in which the lower picture's left-to-right balance changes between two hours of the same night. Settle this before the night picture's own description is refined or re-fitted. **(Version 34: the floor lies below the unlit share, and is not a way of setting the night level.)** The floor is lower than the unlit share the lighting starts from, so wherever either light reaches, through the hours the night picture carries the frame, the lit level is above the floor by itself and the floor has nothing to do there; what it acts on is the upper part of the frame towards either end of the night, where the level's own shaping has already brought the level down. Two consequences for a fit. A night arm whose unlit share has been fitted below its floor is floored over the whole frame at every hour of the night, whatever the floor's own level - the night picture becomes its painted image times one constant, with none of the light and none of the shaping in it - and no reading of the floor's own value will say so, because at that point the floor's value is the only thing left and it fits whatever level the night wants. And since that share is the same one the day picture starts from (see **How the two lights combine**), a fit cannot slide into that state without moving the day picture too, which is what the shared share is for.
    - **Where the shaping up the frame can be read (version 33).** It takes almost nothing out at the dead of night and a great deal at either end of the night, so the dead of night says nothing about it at all. Read it in the shoulders instead - the hours either side of the late evening, and the small hours before dawn - where it is the difference between a night picture that holds its level well up the frame and one that does not. A build whose shaping is fitted to nearly nothing there has the mechanism in name only, and its painted fall will have taken the work over; see the tie of one rate in the height above, which is what settles its strength without a fit of its own.
- **The light (version 19; replaces version 17's description of the arc).** The gradient computation takes a warped hour of day: it advances slowly around midday and quickly around midnight. One angle, advancing with that warped hour and turning once a day, drives two soft lights that brighten the image:
  - a **sun-like spot** whose centre travels a closed oval, once a day, larger than the frame: it comes up past the left edge early in the day, crosses the very top edge of the picture around midday, goes down past the right edge late in the day, and stays below the bottom edge of the picture all night. Its centre is outside the frame for most of the day, so what the picture shows is usually the skirt of the spot entering from an edge or a corner, low and to one side; and around midday, with the centre on the top edge, the whole top band is lifted broadly and evenly rather than showing an oval anywhere. Both coordinates of the centre come from that one angle, which goes round the oval at a steady rate in the warped hour (one coordinate its cosine, the other its sine), so the horizontal motion slows near both side edges as well as lingering around midday where the warp slows it too. How fast the light crosses the picture at a given hour is therefore set by the shape of the oval and by the warp together: the warp decides when the light leaves the frame, the oval decides whether it leaves at all, and neither can be fitted without the other.
  - **How far the spot rises and falls (version 23).** Its vertical travel spans the whole picture: the centre is on the top edge around the warped midday, it crosses the bottom edge towards either warped end of the daylight hours, and in the middle of the night it is far below the picture. A path that keeps the centre part way up the frame at the ends of the day is wrong, and what it spoils is not the bottom corners but the middle of the frame: at those hours the vertical travel is what sets the picture's brightness through its middle band, row by row, with the lowest rows barely affected. The brightest row sits at the very bottom edge at almost every hour whatever the path does, so it cannot pin this; the row-by-row profile across the middle of the frame, read at the ends of the daylight hours, can. **(Version 33.)** Two cautions about fitting it. The travel cannot be read from the level of that profile at one hour, because an exposure change looks much the same there; read the profile at two hours of the same day against each other, with one free scale on the pair, which cancels both the exposure and any fixed row of the painted picture - the pairs that carry it are the ones that straddle the morning rise or the evening fall, while pairs taken within the working day are blind to it. And because this travel is what fixes where the pair of steps turns, it cannot be moved on its own: a change here carries every curve of the clock with it, and that shows at the shoulder hours far more plainly than the travel itself ever shows anywhere. Fit it together with the warp, the step placement and the two lights, never one at a time.
  - a **glow along the bottom edge** whose horizontal centre comes from the same angle mirrored, so it travels the other way, from right to left over the day, at the same uneven pace, and stops short of both side edges instead of leaving the frame. Up the frame it is a soft band whose centre sits close to the bottom edge - in the original a little above it (version 28; version 19 said at or just below, which rules the original's own choice out) - so the brightness is even along the very bottom and then falls away smoothly over the lower part of the picture. It is not a bright rim at the edge with a tail above it.
  - **The glow's width across the frame is not fixed (version 23).** Through most of the daylight hours it keeps a steady width, but around the middle of the day it broadens, spreading its lift along the bottom of the picture instead of gathering it under one part of the width. So at midday the bottom of the picture is flatter from side to side than a glow of fixed width would make it. What a build whose glow keeps one width all day does instead follows from that rather than having been seen on such a build (INFERRED): it gathers the same lift under one part of the width, so it is too bright in the middle of the bottom edge at midday while agreeing at the other hours. What to read from footage: the bottom edge's left-to-right profile at midday, against the same profile at other hours of the same month. **(Version 28: read it strictly.)** Around the middle of the day is to be read strictly: the window is narrow, and a little either side of the warped midday most of the broadening has already gone. A factor of the clock that is still near its widest through the morning and the afternoon is the wrong shape even if it is at its widest at noon, because it spends the broadening on hours that should have none. What pins how narrow it is: the bottom edge's left-to-right profile at a run of hours either side of midday, against the same profile in the middle of the morning and the middle of the afternoon. The same narrow broadening happens once more at the dead of night, for the night image's light on its own unwarped clock, and nowhere else. **(Version 34: no hour a usual set holds can see how narrow the window is, and the width and the broadening cannot be separated at midday.)** The hours a usual set is made of - the quarters of the day, and an hour late in the evening - say nothing whatever about the breadth of that window. At the middle of the day, and again at the dead of night, a narrow window and a much broader one are both at their widest and give the same frame to the last level; at the quarters of the day either side, both have given out and agree again. So a fit over such a set leaves the breadth completely free and will simply keep whatever it was started with, however wrong, and its own error will report nothing. Frames an hour or so either side of the middle of the day have to be asked for, and the breadth fitted on them, or it cannot be fitted at all. Separately, the glow's own width across the frame cannot be told apart from the broadening on frames at the middle of the day alone, because there the bottom band's width is the one times the other: read the profile across the width at an hour near the middle of the day **and** at an hour well away from it, where the width is the glow's own, and fit the two together. A fit given only the one hour will trade them against each other.

  Both lights are **wide and gentle**: each lifts a broad region of the picture, and neither ever reads as a bright patch with an edge to it, at any hour. Their widths and their strengths against each other decide how much of the picture's structure sits at the top and how much at the bottom at a given hour, and they are not matters of the path: at midday, with the spot's centre on the top edge and the glow near the middle of the width, the top of the picture is broad and even and leans to neither side. Away from both lights the image keeps most of its brightness, and neither light takes the picture past the image's own colour: a light brings its area up towards the colour the image already has, never beyond it.

  **How the two lights combine (version 23).** The picture starts from a fixed share of the image's own level, and each light adds its own lift on top of that; where both lights reach the same part of the picture, the two lifts **add**, with nothing taken off for the overlap. The promise that the picture never passes the image's own colour is kept by the strengths themselves - the fixed share and the two lights are chosen so that their total cannot carry the picture past the image's own colour - and not by combining the lights through a rule that returns less than their sum where they overlap. How large the fixed share is, and how much each light can lift, are left to be fitted from footage. In the original the two lights seldom reach the same part of the picture at once: they travel opposite ways across the width, and the spot comes near the bottom of the frame only when it has gone off to one side. So where the widths and the paths are right the choice of rule hardly shows, and where a build's two lights do sit on top of each other for part of the day it has two things to look at rather than one: a rule that returns less than the sum darkens the overlap, and the overlap itself says the widths or the path differ. Both show in the same place - the lower part of the picture at the hours when both lights are low in the frame - so the rule, the widths, the strengths and the oval have to be fitted together rather than one at a time.

  **One share for both arms, and no clamp anywhere (version 34; corrects the sentence above about why the picture never passes the image's own colour).** That fixed share is one and the same for the day picture and for the night picture: both arms start from the same share of their own image's level, and both add the same two lifts, at the same strengths and the same widths. The arms differ in which clock drives their lights and their curves, in the floor under the night level (see **The night images**), and in that on the day arm those same lifts also pale the saturation while on the night arm they act on the night picture's colour as it is. Giving the night arm a share of its own is more freedom than the original has, and it is the freedom through which a whole-frame fit loses its night lighting altogether without any single reading objecting. As for the promise above, it is not the strengths that keep it but the geometry: the two lights are never over the same part of the picture, one travelling across the width as the mirror of the other, so when either is near the middle of the width the other is at the top edge or below the bottom. The most the picture is ever carried to is therefore the share plus a single light's lift, and that stays short of the image's own colour. So there is nothing to clamp, and nothing in the original clamps it. A build that needs a clamp - whose share and lifts together can carry a lit place past the image's own colour - has them too large between them, and wherever that clamp binds it flattens exactly the brightest-lit places, which are the places the lights' own shape would otherwise show in.

  **What pins the fixed share, the two lifts and the paling (version 28).** These can be read separately, and at hours the usual footage already covers, because the share and the lifts act on the brightest of a pixel's three channels while the paling leaves that channel alone. Take the per-pixel largest of the three channels of the background, at a place one of the lights passes over, and compare it with the same place's reading at the hour of the same day when that light has gone to the other side of the width. The painted image cancels between the two hours, because it is the same picture at both, and through the middle of the day the clock's own curves above are flat, so what is left is how far that light carries the brightest channel above the unlit share. Read a place high in the frame for the travelling spot and a place low in the frame for the glow along the bottom edge, each at the hour its light is over it and at the hour it is furthest away. Then, at the same places and hours, the smallest of a pixel's three channels over its largest pins the paling, which is why that reading comes second. Compare a build against the original by these comparisons between hours rather than by the levels at any one hour, since the composite's exposure is not the same in every mood. Two things not to do: do not read where across the width the brightest channel peaks, because that travels over the day in the original as much as in any build and so says nothing; and do not let a fit trade one light's lift against the other's - read each one from the comparison between hours above, or the lift can end up on the wrong light while the picture still agrees at the hour it was fitted at.

  **The balance between the two lifts, read within one frame (version 34).** That last warning needs a reading of its own, and there is one that costs no new footage and no second hour. On a single daytime frame, read each light against the picture away from both: the middle of the top band against the two top corners, and the middle of the bottom band against the two bottom corners, in the same units on the same frame. Re-fitting the palettes, the exposure or the tone curve raises or lowers everything together, so what those two readings pin is the **balance** between the two lifts, which no palette and no exposure can alter. Take them at the hours around the middle of the day, when both lights are in or close to the frame, and at the ends of the day as a check, since that balance holds at every daytime hour. A build whose balance is wrong has moved light from one of its lights to the other; it can still agree with the original over the whole frame at the hour it was fitted at, and it is then wrong in how its error is laid out up the frame at every daytime hour - too much of the picture's structure at the top and too little along the bottom edge, or the other way about. Of everything in this bullet that is still a matter of fitting, this balance is the one worth the most on screen, and the one a whole-frame fit is least likely to find on its own.

  The spot's oval together with the mirrored bottom glow gives the background its structure through the day; a single broad highlight drifting across is not enough.
- **Low resolution, then smooth upscaling.** The gradient is computed into a small off-screen image each frame and stretched over the screen with smooth filtering. The content is soft, so a small image costs almost nothing and looks the same.
- **Easing from frame to frame.** Each frame's new gradient is blended over the previous frame's rather than replacing it, so any change in its inputs is smoothed out over time.

## 4. The wave

### 4.1 Shape and motion

- The wave is a **sheet**: think of a long ribbon of cloth, wider than the screen, seen from a camera below it, looking up at it obliquely (version 17; earlier versions said "slightly raised", which was wrong), so that it crosses the screen from left to right as a band whose thickness and height vary along its length. It folds over itself gently in places, so that parts of it are seen nearly edge-on. (Version 22: neither where the camera stands relative to the sheet, and so how obliquely the sheet is seen, nor where the camera points, is stated anywhere in this spec; both are fitted from footage against the checks in 4.4d, each against its own observable.)
- It spans the full width of the screen with both **ends off screen** (version 17: the shaken end well beyond the left edge, the free end just at or beyond the right edge; see 4.4d). Its band sits around or a little below the vertical middle of the screen (see 4.6), on a gentle arch or S-curve. The curve drifts, but the band stays well inside the screen.
- **More than the bright band (version 22).** What is seen is not the bright band alone. Above it a broad, faint face of the sheet shows much of the time: the part of the sheet's width that recedes from the camera, lit far more weakly than the folds, reading as a soft veil or arc over the band rather than as lines, and at night as a dim veil over a dark sky. A build in which the bright ribbon is all there is, with the background clean right up to its edge, is missing it; see the checks in 4.4d. **How far it reaches (version 26).** It keeps close company with the band: it fades out not far above it and never climbs to the top edge of the picture. Measure that the same way on footage and on your own renders: how far above the band's bright core a column still carries light clearly above the background, and separately how often any of the sheet's light reaches the topmost rows of the frame at all. On footage of the original that second count is nil, at every time of day and at every time since the start. A build that shows a pale wash reaching the top of the frame in a fair share of its renders has not found this faint face but something else; look for it in the states where the band swings highest and furthest, which is where such a wash appears. A wash like that also hides itself from measurement, because a tool that estimates the background from the top and bottom rows of the frame is thrown by light sitting in those rows, and the band's own extent and thickness then read as something they are not.
- **Folded across its width, not a flat strip (version 26).** Across its width the sheet is folded over on itself repeatedly rather than stretched flat, so a column of the picture cuts through more than one layer of cloth. That is what the band is made of: overlapping strands that cross, slide past one another and separate again, dark eye-shaped partings where layers part company, and pinches where a fold turns edge-on. The layers lie at different depths and at different angles to the eye, so they do not move as one piece: neighbouring strands drift past each other, and the fine structure of crossings and partings reshapes within a second or two while the band as a whole sways slowly. It is the whole length of the sheet that is folded this way, not only the part near the shaken end. A band whose strands stay parallel and rise and fall together is a strip rather than a folded sheet, however well it is lit, and the quicker part of its motion is missing with them. 4.4a says where the folding comes from and 4.4d how to check it on your own geometry.
- Its motion is slow and calm: the big shape evolves over tens of seconds while smaller ripples run along the sheet. It never repeats exactly, never settles and never moves abruptly.
- **It keeps its substance as it runs (version 22).** "Never settles" holds over minutes and tens of minutes as well as over seconds. Long after the start the band is still as thick, as uneven in thickness along the screen and as full of strands and dark partings as it was in the first minute: the picture differs from moment to moment, but not in character. The finest and fastest of its motion may ease off a little as the run goes on; the band itself does not draw in. A build whose sheet smooths out as it runs, the band gathering towards a single even rope with its strands merging, is wrong however good its first minute looks. Check it on your own renders at times since the start ranging from well under a minute to tens of minutes, and on footage of the original at the same times, by one and the same method on both: the band's thickness, how much that thickness varies along the screen, and the strands and dark partings in a column. Check at more than one time of day. Where the band is faint over a dark background, a rule that measures how far a column's light extends counts the faint far face above the band as well, and then it is not the band's own thickness that is being compared.
- **Each of those is a series, not a level (version 30).** "Not in character" does not mean "the same". From one moment to another the band's thickness, how much of it pinches to a thin bright line, and where it sits on the screen are each quite different, and the three do not move in step with one another: at some moments a good share of the columns pinch and at others none do at all, and the band can be thin while it rides low or full while it rides high. So none of the three is a single number to hit, and a median over clips is a summary of a series rather than a target in itself. Compare each of them **as a series against time since the start** - the same times on footage of the original and on your own renders, spread from tens of seconds to tens of minutes, by one and the same method on both - and look at the spread as well as the middle. A build whose band is the same thickness at every moment, or which never pinches at any of them, is missing this even when its median thickness is right; and because tuning footage is usually weighted towards one time since the start, such a build can be right at that one moment and wrong at every other, in either direction.
- **How it ends at the free-end edge (version 30).** The band does not run out of the picture at full strength. Towards the free-end edge of the frame it thins, pinches and dims, because the sheet itself is running out there: its free end lies at or a little beyond that edge (4.4d) and the fade the sheet carries at its ends (4.4c) is already biting inside the picture. At those moments when the band pinches at all, that is where most of the pinching is. A build whose band keeps its thickness and its brightness right up to that edge has its free end further out of the frame than the original's, or no end fade reaching the frame, or both. Measure it the same way on footage and on your own renders: the band's thickness and its mean light in the free-end tenth of the width, against the same in the middle third.
- The surface looks perfectly smooth at any resolution (no visible facets or kinks).
- The animation advances with real time (independent of the frame rate) and stays stable whatever the frame rate.

### 4.2 Geometry (required)

- The wave is a genuine **three-dimensional surface** (two parameters: along and across the ribbon) that moves in 3D, rendered through a **perspective camera**. A screen-space curve or band that only imitates depth does not meet this requirement.
- Perspective is visible: the parts of the sheet nearer the camera look larger, and the spacing of its shading detail changes with distance and with the angle at which the sheet is seen.
- Brightness depends on the real angle between the surface and the line of sight (edge-on brighter, facing the camera fainter), computed from the 3D surface.
- The sheet can genuinely fold and pass in front of or behind other parts of itself; where layers overlap they add up (the wave never hides anything, section 2).

### 4.3 Look

- **Apparent thickness.** The band is broad where the sheet faces the viewer and pinches to a thin bright line where the sheet turns edge-on.
- **Strands.** The band often reads as two or three overlapping strands that cross and separate along the screen.
- **Silk, not separate threads.** The sheet reads as smooth translucent silk or satin, with soft streaks running along its length. The streaks are finest at the fringes of the band and where it pinches. At normal viewing resolutions individual threads are not distinguishable.
- **Translucent faces.** Where the sheet faces the viewer it is faint and see-through: the background shows through, only slightly lifted.
- **Brightest** along the folds where the sheet is edge-on, and where several layers overlap.
- **Colour.** The wave adds near-neutral white light, so over the background it reads as a pale, lighter version of the background's hue. It is never saturated. It is dimmer at night.
- **Edges.** The ends and the long edges of the sheet fade out smoothly; no hard edge ever shows.
- **Never jagged.** No shimmering, crawling or stair steps at any resolution, including when the sheet is nearly edge-on.

### 4.4 Construction: simulation, surface, warp and camera

The original builds the sheet in four stages. Do the same, with your own values.

**a. A spring-mass control grid (the motion).**

- A coarse square grid of control points (section 11) is simulated as a **mass-spring net**, the textbook cloth model. Each point is joined by springs to its immediate neighbours along both grid directions (structural springs, rest length = the grid spacing). It is also joined to the points two away along both directions (bending springs, rest length twice the spacing, stiffer), which keeps the sheet smooth instead of crumpled.
- **Relative stiffness (version 9).** The springs across the width are comparable in stiffness to those along the length, not markedly softer. The bending springs are clearly stiffer than the direct ones (version 17: much stiffer, not just somewhat). On screen, this arrangement makes the picture hold together longer from one second to the next. (Version 30: in a net of this kind the stiffnesses are a lever on the **size** of the shape the net takes and hardly at all on the **pace** at which it changes; see "What the rates cannot give" in (b) before reaching for them to speed anything up.)
- **Damping (version 13):** each step, every point's velocity is reduced in proportion to itself. The damping is **light**: motion brought in at the shaken edge keeps going for longer than a full cycle of the drive, so a fold launched at the shaken end is still clearly travelling when it reaches the free end. "Longer than a full cycle of the drive" means longer than the **slower** of the shaken edge's two motions: roughly two of its cycles (version 14).
- **Disturbance:** each step, every point gets a small random kick to its velocity in all three axes, from a deterministic pseudo-random generator. This keeps the motion alive and never repeating. Each kick is tiny next to the shaken edge's own motion. Because the damping is light, their effect builds up into a fine, ever-changing agitation everywhere on the sheet, comparable to the motion the drive brings in; that is what keeps the detail alive (version 13).
- **Which points are held.** One end of the sheet is driven. The last point of every row along the length is placed kinematically (its velocity is zeroed) on a slow oscillating path in height and depth. **All rows share that one path (version 9).** The driven points are not spread across the sheet's width at their own rest positions: every row's driven point lies on the same path, only earlier or later along it. The driven end therefore behaves like a single point moving along a path, which gathers that end of the sheet, so the sheet stays bunched across its width instead of lying spread out flat. The height and depth use two different frequencies that do not divide each other, and the phase changes gradually from row to row, so the driven end twists and undulates. Motion runs from that end along the sheet as travelling waves. The other end is free, but each step it gets a steady gentle push along the length, which keeps the sheet extended like a flag in a light breeze. The time that drives the end is itself eased (it follows the simulation clock smoothly), so the driven end never jumps.
- **Folded slack across its width (version 26).** At rest the net is a square grid with the same spacing both ways (above), so it carries comparably much cloth across its width as along its length. What is drawn from it is a long ribbon, far longer than it is wide. So the cloth laid across the width is much longer than the straight line between the width's edges: the width cannot lie flat, and it does not. It hangs in folds over the whole of the length, not only near the shaken end, and that folding is what the band is made of - the overlapping layers, crossings, dark partings and edge-on pinches of 4.1, and the broad faint face beside the band with them.
  - **Slack, not squeezed.** This is not cloth under compression. The springs across the width sit at about their rest length, neither stretched nor squashed: the width is simply slack and crumpled, and it stays so because nothing in (a) pulls it anywhere else - no gravity, no rest shape, no flat plane - while the bending springs keep the folds broad and smooth rather than finely crumpled. Along the length it is the other way about: the steady push at the free end, working against the held end, pulls the length nearly straight. The two directions of the same net are therefore quite unlike each other, and a build whose width is as taut as its length is drawing a strip rather than a sheet.
  - **What holds it folded.** Three things together: one whole end gathered onto the single shared path above, which bunches the width and leaves it bunched because nothing further along straightens it; nothing pulling any point towards a rest position (see 4.4e); and light damping, so the folds a gathered end makes persist and travel instead of dying out. Two things it is **not**. It is not the random nudges shaking a taut sheet: take them away and the width is as folded as before. And it is not set by the springs' rest lengths - a net of this kind settles into much the same folded width over a wide range of them, so they are the wrong lever and changing them scales the whole sheet instead. What decides whether the width is folded at all is whether the net's own full width of cloth is what reaches the screen as the sheet's width, whether one whole end is gathered, and whether anything pulls the net flat.
  - **Check it on your own geometry.** By one and the same measure both ways: the length of the path the net follows across its width, against the straight line between the width's edges; and the same along its length. The two come out quite unlike each other, nearly equal along the length and far apart across the width. Then, as 4.4d sets out: how many times the width turns over down one column of the screen, which is the most telling of these; how much of the depth the sheet's width could occupy the drawn surface actually occupies; and the spread of the view-angle cosine of 4.5.
  - **Where the excess has to go.** Into the depth the sheet's width occupies, layer over layer, each layer at its own depth and its own angle to the eye. Two other ways of arriving at a band that looks wide are wrong, and the checks in 4.4d will catch both: stretching the width when the net is mapped into the scene, which spreads the same taut cloth over more depth and never folds it; and winding the width into a tight scroll at nearly one depth, turn packed against turn, which reads on screen as a narrow fused band of parallel strands.
  - **How much, and how to fit it.** This spec gives no value for how much more cloth the width holds than the width it shows. It is a strong lever on the band, and it moves the same things the envelope's level below moves, so fit the two together rather than one after the other. Measure, on footage of the original and on your own renders of the same conditions, by one and the same method on both: how thick the band is, how much that thickness varies from place to place along the screen, how many strands and dark partings a column shows, and how differently neighbouring strands move. Two cautions while you do. Keep the sheet's size and the drive's size in proportion: making the sheet smaller while leaving the drive as it was speeds up everything on screen - the surface's own detail, and the sparkles of section 6 that ride on its motion - and how fast the surface's detail travels across the screen is an observable in its own right, to be measured on footage and kept where footage puts it. (Version 30: that speed, the band's size on screen and the ratio between them all swing widely from one moment to another with nothing changed at all, so pool them over many moments before reading a shortfall into them; on single clips the ratio's own swing is as large as the differences people try to fit with it.) And do not look for the band's substance in a larger or faster shaken edge instead (see 4.4e), which goes wrong the same way.
- **Envelope along the length.** Before the net is used, its points' height and depth are scaled by a smooth envelope along the length: subdued over one part of the sheet, fuller over another. **What is scaled (version 18).** The coordinates themselves, about one fixed axis running along the sheet's length, the same axis for every point. It is a plain scaling of the shape the net has arrived at: it varies how much of that shape, its folds, its gathering across the width and its undulation, is expressed from place to place along the length, and it never moves a point towards anything. No rest shape, flat plane, regular grid or even spacing takes any part in it; in particular, do not read "scaled" as a blend between each point and a rest position (see 4.4e). Scaling a point's departure from an evenly spaced rest grid does something else entirely: it spreads the sheet out across its width, undoing the gathering that the one shared driven path produces, and the level below can then not be fitted at all, because raising it opens the sheet into separate ribbons with gaps between them instead of showing more of the net's own shape. **Which way round (version 17):** it is fullest near the shaken end, which is mostly off screen; a little way along it dips; over the rest of the sheet, which includes everything visible, it settles at a moderate, nearly even level. The crossover point shifts slightly with the time-of-day mood (towards the shaken end at dusk and night). **How full it is over the visible sheet (version 18).** That level decides how much of the net's own shape reaches the screen, and it is a strong lever on what the band looks like: how thick it is, how much its thickness varies from place to place along the screen, how many strands and dark partings a column shows, and how differently neighbouring strands move. This spec gives no value for it. Measure those four things on footage of the original and on your own renders of the same conditions, by one and the same method on both, and fit the level so that they match. (Version 26: fit it together with the folding of the width, above, since the two move the same four things.) **What moves together with it (version 22).** Changing the level alone, up and down, with nothing else altered, is also a check that the envelope is applied as this bullet describes. As the level rises the band thickens, and a column shows more strands and more dark partings, not fewer; the broad sheen inside the faces (4.5) does not widen with it; and the band's brightness over the background stays in the region the original's own does. A build in which raising the level instead gathers the band into one brighter, more solid ribbon with fewer strands and a sheen far broader than the original's is telling you that something besides the level changed, or that the envelope is not applied as this bullet describes: find that before fitting any further. And while you fit the level against the four things above, keep watching the strand count, the width of the band's broad sheen and its brightness over the background, because a level fitted against thickness alone can take all three a long way out, and the look of the band with them. **Where to look when it comes out the other way (version 26).** The likeliest cause is a width that is not folded as the bullet above requires: either flat and taut, or wound into a tight scroll at nearly one depth. A scroll's turns all grow together as the level rises, which widens the band's broad sheen and closes its partings instead of adding strands and partings. So when raising the level widens the sheen and loses partings, look first at how the width is folded, and only then at the level. **Its shape over the visible sheet is not a lever (version 30).** The level is; the gentle rise and dip of the profile from one side of the frame to the other is not. Flattening that variation, or moving its dip out of the frame, changes nothing that can be measured in the band - not its thickness anywhere along the screen, not its pinching, not its centre line, not how fast the surface travels in one part of the screen against another. So do not look to the profile's shape to put a pattern into the band along the screen, and do not read a flat-looking profile over your own frame as a fault.
- **Time step.** The net is integrated with a **fixed time step** (semi-implicit Euler is enough): velocity from forces, then position from velocity, then damping. Real elapsed time (capped, so that a stall or a hidden tab does not produce a huge catch-up) is accumulated, and whole fixed steps are taken while enough has built up. For drawing, the net is **interpolated** between the previous and the current step by the leftover fraction, so the motion is smooth at any frame rate. ("Fix your timestep" with interpolation.) Step rate: section 11.
- **Start.** The original starts from a stored, already-settled state. Pre-run your net (section 9) so that the first frame already looks like a typical running state. **The same at every start (version 18).** Nothing about the original's start is drawn at random. Two runs begun at the same date and time of day and watched for the same length of time show the same picture frame for frame: the same band height, the same arch, the same place for the hump of (b). Every part of the warp in (b) reads the one simulation clock, which starts from a fixed value, and the small random nudges above come from a deterministic generator started from a fixed point, so the picture at a given time since the start is fixed. Do not draw the clock's starting value, the phases of the warp's parts or the length of the warm-up at random per run. The variety the original shows from moment to moment comes from how long it has been running, not from a seed: the sheet's motion and the warp at a given time since the start should not depend on the seed of your render interface (section 9), and different moments of the wave are sampled by varying the time since the start. **A changed sheet needs a new warm-up (version 26).** A net whose folding or size has been changed is no longer the net the stored state came from, and it takes a long while to find its own settled state - far longer than the warm-ups that serve an unchanged net. Until it has, everything on screen moves faster than it will when it is settled. So measure a changed sheet only from a state that has stopped drifting, and show that it has: the same geometry checks above, taken at times since the start well apart from each other, should have stopped trending.

**b. The slow large-scale warp (free-form deformation).**

- The whole net is passed through a **free-form deformation lattice**: a coarse 3D lattice with more cells along the length than across or in depth, spanning the sheet's region. Each control point is mapped into the lattice's normalised coordinates (an offset and a scale). It is then displaced by a **tricubic B-spline** blend of the lattice's displacements and scaled again into the scene. **Enough cells, and held at the ends (version 18):** across the width the lattice needs enough cells to carry the one-cycle twist described below. Where the blend has too few spans across the width to represent it, the twist collapses into the simplest shape those spans can hold, and the band loses that fine structure across its own width. (Version 22: version 18 also said the band "can no longer bow and tilt across its own width". That overstates it and is withdrawn: what the spans across the width govern is the fine structure within the band, not where the band points. The band's lean across the screen is carried by the crest of the swell travelling across it, so do not look to the lattice's spans across the width to cure a band that leans steadily one way; see 4.4d.) And where a control point falls at or beyond the end of the lattice, hold the displacement at the value it has there, rather than letting the warp's functions go on growing outside the sheet.
- The lattice displacement is **vertical only** (the uneven layout below is fixed and separate): a smooth curve along the length that varies somewhat with depth. That is what gives the band its overall arch or S-shape and makes it drift up and down.
- **What drives it (version 11).** The displacement at each lattice cell is the sum of four parts. Each is a smooth function of the cell's position and of one slowly advancing clock that runs with the simulation:
  - **A wave rolling along the length.** It runs from the shaken end towards the free end, with a little more than one wavelength along the sheet. Its height grows steadily along the length, so it is a few times taller at the free end than at the shaken end. Its overall strength also swells and fades. **How fast its strength changes (version 26):** appreciably within the time one crest takes to cross the screen, so that no two crossings of the band are alike. Where that strength only creeps while a crest travels, each crossing is a copy of the last and the band's rise and fall repeats itself, which shows up as a picture a few seconds later that is too exactly the reverse of the picture now. This is about the band's course over seconds repeating itself, not about its quick detail; for that see "How fast the four parts run" below. This is the swell of 4.6: its crest crossing the screen is the band's slow left-to-right rise and fall, and everything on the band (strands, folds, partings) is carried along with it.
  - **A sliding bump.** One soft hump, roughly a quarter of the sheet's length across, slides slowly back and forth along the length, not round in a loop. For part of its travel it is beyond the shaken end and has no effect. Over the rest of its travel it passes over every part of the sheet, including the part at the right of the screen (version 26); what it leaves where it is is a slow change of the band's height, not a stirring of the surface there.
  - **A slow bob.** The whole sheet rises and falls together, much more slowly than the swell.
  - **A small twist.** A gentle ripple, one cycle across the sheet's width, travels across the width and tilts the band slightly.

  The displacement is computed directly from the clock each step, not eased towards targets; it is smooth because every part is slow.
- **How large (version 17).** The bump and the bob are not subtle details. On screen each, on its own, moves the visible band up and down by a clearly visible part of the band's own thickness. Together with the swell they make the warp the largest motion of the band over minutes. The swell is the fastest of the four parts and the twist the next; the bob is much slower, and the bump slower still.
- **Checking the bump and the bob (version 18).** Neither of them can be seen in a short clip. Over a few seconds the band's height is carried by the swell, and a build whose bump and bob are far too weak measures much the same as a correct one, so a short clip cannot tell them apart and is no evidence that they are right. Check them only on footage long enough for the band's slow rise and fall to come round more than once, comparing the original and your own build at the same conditions on: the band's mean height over the whole clip, its total range, how much of its slow motion sits at the longest periods rather than at the swell's own, and the up-and-down swing of the band's centre at a fixed place across the screen.
- **What their sizes settle, and what they do not (version 26).** They settle how far the band wanders up and down over minutes; they do not settle where it sits. The bob does its work bodily: at any one moment it lifts or lowers the whole width by the same amount, so it moves the band without changing its shape, and it is the main carrier of the band's slow wandering over minutes - almost all of the band's slow motion at the longest periods is its doing. The bump acts where it happens to be, so its effect is uneven along the screen, but what it leaves there is a slow change of height rather than a local stirring. Therefore: if your band's wandering over many times since the start has the original's spread but not its typical height, that is a matter for the camera's aim (4.4d) or for the envelope's level in (a), and not for the size of a slow part. Enlarging these to lift the band would take the spread out in the same move, and the spread is the thing they govern. **The other way round, too (version 30).** Now the case that is easier to get wrong. A band that sits at about the original's typical height when many moments are averaged, but that is too near the middle of its own range at the extreme moments - not far enough down the screen where the original's band is at its lowest, not far enough up where the original's is at its highest - has slow parts that are too small, and its aim is not at fault. Aiming the camera there would put one sampled moment right and take every other further out. The two faults look alike whenever the footage is weighted towards one time since the start, as tuning footage usually is, so compare the whole course and not the median: **the spread over many times since the start first, the typical height afterwards.** Fit the sizes before the aim, and check the aim again after any change to them. Two further things to know while you do it. Almost the whole of the wandering is the bob's: a build short of reach whose course still follows the original's shape is short in that one part, and the bump will not make it up. And the wandering is a recurring rise and fall on the timescale 4.6 calls "over minutes", with next to nothing in it slower than that - so sampling the band's height at times spread over tens of minutes is sampling one recurring motion at scattered phases, not following a drift, and a fit that treats it as a drift will not come right.
- **How fast the four parts run (version 18).** Their rates do not only govern the band's slow wandering. Because the warp carries the whole sheet bodily through the frame, they also govern how quickly the band's picture changes from one second to the next: how much of the band's detail is still in place a second later, how much of the band's motion sits at the quicker end of its spectrum rather than the slow end, and how long dark partings last. How fast the net itself is stirred has far less effect on those three than it appears to: with the warp's rates held, pacing the net up or down barely moves them. So read those three as evidence about the warp, not about the net. This spec gives no rates. Measure them on footage of the original and on your own renders of the same conditions, by one and the same method on both, together with how long a crest takes to cross the screen, and fit the four parts' rates so that they match, keeping the ordering above. If what you measure disagrees with a timescale stated elsewhere in this spec, report the disagreement rather than quietly splitting the difference. (Version 30: version 18's list also named the period over which the band tilts back and forth. **Strike it.** In the original that period is the crest of the swell crossing the screen - 4.4d says as much about the lean - so it is the swell's own period under a second name, and fitting another part to it makes that part as fast as the swell and breaks the ordering this bullet asks for.) **Phases are not the place to fit (version 30).** The warp's parts are plain functions of the one clock, and the band's whole course over tens of minutes follows from their rates and sizes and from where the clock starts; "The same at every start" in (a) says this from the other side. So fit the rates and the sizes against the **whole course**: the same times since the start on footage of the original and on your own renders, spread from tens of seconds to tens of minutes, compared as two series rather than at any one moment. And if a free phase has had to be given to each part separately to bring individual moments into line, suspect the rates before the phases: a set of free phases will line up a few sampled moments while the course between them, and the size of it, stay wrong, which is what a course of the right shape with too little reach looks like. **What the rates cannot give, and what pins them (version 26).** Version 18's "to a large extent", above, is too strong and is withdrawn, and so is the move it invites. Of the four parts, the rolling swell carries nearly all of the warp's own rate of change; the other three could be stopped altogether without the pace of the picture changing much, so there is little to fit in them against anything that happens within a second. And the swell's rate is not a free lever: a rate and a wavelength come as a pair, and what footage shows of the pair is the time a crest takes to cross the screen. So fit that crossing time, and once it agrees, leave the swell's rate where it is; do not then move it to chase how much of the band's detail survives a second, or how much of the motion sits at the quicker end of its spectrum. A sweep of all the rates together by one common factor cannot settle this either: no factor matches both how much detail survives a second and how far the band's centre moves in a second, and a sweep that cannot match two measurements at once is evidence about the sweep. **But the quick end does come from this clock (version 30).** Version 26 went on to say that no part of the warp is anywhere near fast enough to put anything at the quick end of what the band shows, and that the quicker part is made by the band's own structure sweeping across the pixels instead. The second half is right; the first is wrong and is withdrawn. Hold the warp still and let the net run on, and the picture very nearly stops renewing: most of the change from one second to the next, and most of what sits at the quick end of the band's spectrum, is the crest of the swell sweeping the sheet past the camera. So the renewal needs **both**, and they are different halves. The crest is the carrier: it moves the sheet's structure across the pixels. The structure is what makes the movement show: broad, smooth, fused light renews slowly however fast it is swept, because moving it changes little. What the net itself carries is neither of those - it carries the difference between the speeds of neighbouring strands and the fast tail of the flow, which are not the renewal at all. Three consequences. If your crest crosses the screen in the time footage gives and your swell is the right size on screen, and the picture still renews too slowly, the clocks are not the place to look: the band's light is too broadly and too smoothly organised, which is the structure of (a) and 4.1 and the shading of 4.5. Do not reach for the springs either: in a net of this kind the stiffness is a lever on the size of the shape the net takes and hardly at all on its pace, so stiffening everything buys a larger sheet and not a quicker one. And do not buy the quick end with a faster clock, which loses the crossing time, the one thing here that footage pins directly. **Two observables, not one (version 30).** A rate and a wavelength are a pair, and the crossing time alone cannot pin two numbers - a build with the rate right and the wavelength long gets the crossing time wrong, which is the easiest of all these faults to misread as a clock running fast. The second observable is how much the band rises and falls **in one piece** across the screen. The crest is a travelling wave, so the band's height at one place across the screen leads or lags its height at another: follow the band's height in several bands of columns across the frame, and the lag that best lines those bands up is the crossing time while their correlation at no lag at all says how much of a wavelength the frame spans. Measure both, by one and the same method on footage and on your own renders. A band that rises and falls too much as one piece while its crest crosses too fast has too long a wavelength, not too fast a clock. Two cautions. The slow parts that lift the whole sheet raise the no-lag correlation as well - take the bob away on any build and it falls at every moment - so fit their sizes first and read this measure afterwards. And a clip has to be appreciably longer than the crossing time for the lag to mean anything at all; on clips shorter than that, both halves of this measurement are noise.
- **The clock does not run for ever (version 30).** The clock that every part of the warp reads is not unbounded. It runs up to a fixed ceiling and restarts from its starting value, and because all the parts read it directly they all jump together when it does. It shows, and it is the one abrupt thing in the whole effect. Within a single frame the band's height steps by a visible fraction of the frame; the frame-to-frame change of the band's centre at that moment is far larger than at any other; the picture a second later is briefly *less* like the picture now than at any other time, the one-second correlation turning negative where it is otherwise positive; and the surface's motion is left raised for a short while afterwards before everything returns to its usual bounds. The reason it is a jolt and not merely a jump is that the driven edge is not set down at a new place: it is swept rapidly along its usual path over a fraction of a second, which throws a real disturbance into the sheet, and that disturbance then travels and dies away like any other. Two things follow for anyone fitting (b). The course after a restart is not the continuation of the course before it: the same build with and without the restart runs identically up to it and then differs, moment by moment, by as much as the whole course's own spread. So a set of slow rates fitted to moments spread over tens of minutes **cannot** be brought onto the later ones unless the restart is there too. And this spec gives no ceiling. It is long enough that a short clip almost never contains one, and it is a value to fit, in the same fit as the rates and sizes above and against the same series: with the restart in, the course of the band's height against time since the start is what pins all of them together.
- **Uneven layout along the length (version 11).** The lattice's cells are not evenly spaced along the length. The part of the sheet near the shaken end is compressed and the part near the free end is spread out many times more, so the free end of the net covers far more of the scene. This layout is fixed and does not move. **What it shows on screen (version 26, corrected in version 30).** The observable stands, and it is a strong one: measure the speed at which the surface's detail travels in the shaken-end third of the screen and in the free-end third separately, on footage of the original and on your own renders of the same conditions, by one and the same method on both, with the sparkles kept out of both sides as far as each side allows. The original's free-end third is the faster at almost every moment. Version 26 then told you to lay the length out less evenly if your two thirds travel at much the same speed, and that is too simple, in a way that matters. **Read the layout on the screen, not in the sheet's own coordinates.** The free end is the further from the camera, so perspective shrinks it: a unit of the sheet's material covers fewer pixels there for that reason alone, and the layout has to spread the free end by more than enough to cancel that before anything shows. So the number to check is not how much the lattice's cells grow from one end of the sheet to the other, nor even how much they grow over the part in frame, but **how many screen pixels one step of the sheet's material covers at the two edges of the frame**. Print that from your own geometry and check that the free-end edge is the larger. Two builds whose layouts grow by the same factor along the sheet can have opposite gradients on screen if their cameras differ, so this is as much a camera check as a layout one (4.4d), and a layout that looks generous in the sheet's coordinates can still arrive at the screen reversed. What an under-spread layout looks like is worth knowing, because it is not simply a missing difference: evening the layout out does not flatten the two thirds towards each other, it **turns them over**, making the shaken-end third faster than the free-end third. So a build whose shaken-end third is too fast *and* whose free-end third is too slow has one fault and not two, and this is where to look for it. Two things it is not. It is not the envelope's profile along the length in (a), which can be flattened altogether without moving either third. And it is not the sliding bump: its travel does take it over the free end, but what it leaves there is a slow change of height, and removing it altogether takes nothing out of the free end's faster travel.
- The lattice's scales vary slightly with the time-of-day mood, so the sheet's proportions change subtly over the day.

**c. The smooth surface.**

- The (deformed) net is the control net of a **uniform bicubic B-spline surface**: each 4 x 4 block of neighbouring control points defines one patch (the textbook arrangement), so the surface is smooth (curvature-continuous) everywhere. It approximates the net and does not pass through its points.
- Each patch is **sampled on a regular grid** in parameter space. All patches use the same number of samples, enough that the facets never show. At each sample the original evaluates the position and the **normal**, as the cross product of the two partial derivatives (left unnormalised: its length is the area of the surface element). It then builds an indexed triangle-strip mesh over all samples. **Comparably fine both ways (version 26).** Sample each patch comparably finely across the ribbon and along it, rather than many times more finely along the length than across it. The structure that 4.1 and (a) put across the width - the folds, the overlapping layers, and the turns where the sheet goes edge-on - is as fine as anything along the length, and a coarse across direction either loses it or shows it as facets. The shading quantities of 4.5 are carried per sample, so the sampling also sets how much the shading is smoothed in each direction. **And no finer than it needs to be (version 30).** Because of that last point the sampling is not free at the other end either: a mesh far finer than the surface needs changes the scale over which the shading is smoothed, and with it the width of the broad sheen and of the bright lines, which 4.5 and 4.6 pin. Settle the sampling by looking for facets at full resolution, in the places where they would show first - where the sheet turns edge-on - and stop there. Whenever you change it in either direction, measure the width of the broad sheen and of the brightest line before and after, and treat a change in those as a cost of the new sampling rather than as something to fit away elsewhere.
- **Edge fade:** every sample also carries a weight that is 1 over most of the sheet and falls smoothly to 0 at both ends and along both long edges. It multiplies the sheet's light. (Version 17: the fall is linear and covers the same fraction of the sheet's extent at the ends and along the long edges.)
- **Where the fade at the free end falls in the picture (version 30).** How far the fade at the sheet's free end reaches into the frame is not a free choice. Together with where that end lies relative to the edge of the frame, it is what makes the band thin, pinch and dim as it leaves the picture there, and the original's band does all three. See 4.1, "How it ends at the free-end edge", for what to measure on footage, and 4.4d for the framing it goes with.
- The original deforms and projects the control points before it evaluates the surface, so the surface, its derivatives and the normal it shades with all live in the projected space. **Version 16: do the same.** Evaluating in world or view space and projecting afterwards gives a different shading angle (see 4.5, "Brightness and view angle"), so it is no longer equally acceptable.

**d. The camera.**

- A **perspective camera** with a moderate vertical field of view, rotated about a tilted axis so that the sheet is seen obliquely. The near and far planes are ordinary.
- **Where the sheet lies (version 16; version 17 makes it checkable).** The sheet's length runs across the view, very nearly at right angles to the line of sight: the camera is not turned sideways along the sheet. Its width recedes from the camera. Check on your own geometry:
  - **Framing.** A large part of the sheet's length, from the shaken end, lies beyond the left edge of the screen; the free end reaches just to the right edge. A good part of the sheet's surface is outside the frame at any time. (Version 30: "just to the right edge" is loose, and two observables tighten it. How the band ends at that edge - 4.1, "How it ends at the free-end edge" - says how far past the edge the sheet's end lies and how much of its end fade is inside the picture; and the on-screen reading of the layout in (b) is measured at the two edges of this same frame, so where the frame sits along the sheet is part of that fit too.)
  - **Depth.** From the left edge of the screen to the right, the distance to the sheet grows only slightly; the left (shaken) side is the nearer.
  - **Depth across the band (version 22).** Down one column of the screen, across the band, the distance to the sheet changes considerably more than it does from the left edge of the screen to the right: it is the sheet's width that recedes, and a good part of that recession is inside the frame. A build whose visible surface spans almost no depth at all is showing a strip rather than a sheet, and the view-angle check below will not come right for it however its camera is placed. (Version 26: this has to come about through the sheet's own folding across its width - see (a) - and not by bringing the camera in towards the sheet, which would satisfy this check while disturbing the ones above and changing every angle the sheet is seen at.)
  - **From below.** The camera is below the sheet's mean plane, looking up at it, so the far side of the sheet appears lower on screen than the near side. Earlier versions said "slightly raised" and "a modest elevation": that was wrong. The angle changes as the sheet swings, from nearly grazing to modest. (Version 22: version 17's "a little below" and "at a small angle" are withdrawn. This spec states no value for how far below the sheet's mean plane the camera stands, nor for how obliquely the sheet is seen as a result. Fit that from footage, against the checks in this subsection, as the next bullet sets out.)
- **Two separate choices, and the observable that fits each (version 22).** Keep two things about the camera apart, here and everywhere below. Where the camera **stands** relative to the sheet is one: call it the camera's placement, meaning how far below the sheet's mean plane it sits and how far from the sheet it is. Where the camera **points** is the other: call it the camera's aim. They are fitted against different observables, and neither is a substitute for the other.
  - **The placement sets the angles the sheet is seen at.** Under 4.5 the angle at which a bit of sheet is seen is taken from the direction from the camera to that bit, so it follows from where the camera stands and not at all from where the camera points. Fit the placement against the framing and depth checks above and the view-angle check below.
  - **The aim sets where the band falls on the screen.** Where what is seen lands in the frame, and so where the band's centre line sits and how high its highest part reaches, is the aim's to settle; once the framing above is right it is a free choice of its own, and neither the placement nor how wide the lens is settles it. So fit the aim directly and separately: measure the band's centre line across the screen, and how high its highest part reaches, on footage of the original and on your own renders of the same conditions, by one and the same method on both, at several times of day and several times since the start, and point the camera so that they agree. (Version 26: at **many** times since the start, from tens of seconds to tens of minutes, and against the typical height over all of them rather than one or two moments; the band wanders widely over minutes under the warp of (b), so two moments cannot tell an aim apart from where the wandering happens to be. See "What their sizes settle" in (b): a course with the original's spread at the wrong height is the aim's business, or the envelope's level, and not a slow part's size.) (Version 30: and fit the sizes of the warp's slow parts **before** the aim, not after, for the reason given in "The other way round, too" in (b) - a course of the right typical height with too little reach is their business and not the aim's, and an aim fitted against a short course has to be undone again as soon as the reach is put right.)
  - **Do not lift or lower the band by re-placing the camera.** Moving the camera does shift the band, but in the same move it changes the angles the sheet is seen at and disturbs the framing and depth checks; aiming the camera puts the band where it belongs and leaves all of those alone.
- **One tilt, not two (version 22).** The aim is in effect a single rotation of the view about the horizontal axis that runs along the sheet's length; that is what the first bullet's "rotated about a tilted axis" comes to in practice. A turn about the vertical axis, swinging the view round to look along the sheet from one side, and any roll about the line of sight, are at most traces beside it; rotations of comparable size are wrong even when the framing they produce is right. It shows on screen. The original's band leans both ways as it moves (4.6) and comes out near level when its slope across the screen is averaged over a clip and over clips, whereas a view that is turned or rolled leaves the band running downhill the same way at every moment and at every time of day. Measure the band's slope across the screen on footage of the original and on your own renders of the same conditions, by one and the same method on both, and keep the turn and the roll small enough that the averages agree. Two cautions on measuring it. The lean swings back and forth as the crest of the swell travels across the screen, crossing level each time, so measure it over footage long enough to cover more than one of those swings: over a shorter clip the average depends on where in the swing the clip happens to fall, by as much as the lean you are trying to measure. And a steady lean, the same way at every moment, cannot come from anything that travels across the sheet's width, since such a thing changes sign as it travels and averages to nothing over its own cycle. Nor is it cured by the camera's placement or by the rotation about the length axis: both move the lean the other way, and besides, the placement is already fixed by the checks above and that rotation by the band's measured centre line. A steady lean is the turn's or the roll's doing, and that is where to look for it.
- **The far width shows (version 22).** The camera sees a good part of the sheet's receding width, not only the near folds that make up the bright band: that is where the broad faint face above the band in 4.1 comes from. Check it the same way on footage and on your own renders: column by column, compare the extent of the column that carries light clearly above the background with the extent of its bright core alone, and compare the two sides both on how often the first is much the larger and on how much larger it is. At a fixed framing, how wide the lens is and how far the camera stands from the sheet have little to do with this. What decides it is how much of the sheet's width lies inside the frame and how much the sheet rises, falls and folds across that width. So where your build shows the bright band alone, with the background clean right above it, look there, and at the shading of 4.5, rather than at where the camera stands or points. **What to expect of it, and how to measure it (version 26).** It is weak and it keeps close to the band; 4.1 bounds how far it reaches and says what a pale wash climbing to the top of the frame means instead. It is also delicate to measure, because it joins the band's own faint skirt: it separates only under a rule that counts light clearly above the background at the column's own scale and bridges small gaps along the column, and it shows most plainly by day. Where a measurement of it finds next to nothing on either side, widen the rule until it finds the original's before concluding anything about your own build.
- **How the sheet is seen (version 16).** Over the visible sheet the surface tilts to the line of sight are spread widely, from edge-on to face-on, and a typical bit is seen obliquely, about halfway between the two. A sheet that is mostly seen near face-on, with only the edges of its folds turning edge-on, is wrong: under the shading of 4.5 its faces then carry no sheen and its light collapses into thin lines, and the faint far face of 4.1 never appears. Check this on your own geometry: the distribution, over the on-screen surface, of the cosine defined in 4.5. **What this check is about (version 22).** It is a check on the sheet, not on the camera. So long as the framing and depth checks above hold, where the camera points and how far it stands from the sheet hardly shift this distribution, and the level of the envelope in (a) hardly shifts it either; only standing well off the sheet's plane would, and such a placement fails the checks above. What the distribution measures is how the sheet itself reaches the picture: how much of its width is in frame, and how much that width rises, falls and folds across itself. A visible sheet that is nearly flat across its width, little more than a long thin strip, cannot show the spread the original does, whatever camera is pointed at it. So read a build that fails this check as a sheet that is not gathered and folded as (a) and 4.1 require, or whose width barely reaches the frame, and look for the cause there. **Two corrections (version 26).** First, version 22 also named how wide the lens is among the things that hardly shift this distribution, and that was wrong: the angle of 4.5 is taken after projection, and the projection is scaled by the lens, so a wider lens does move the whole distribution. That does not make the lens the cure. It moves every angle at once and takes the framing, the band's size on screen and the measurements of 4.6 with it, and this check is about the sheet; if a wider lens is what brings your distribution into range, read that as a sheet too flat across its width. Second, the distribution does not follow from how thick the band is, nor from how much depth the band covers: a thin band whose width turns over repeatedly down a column shows the original's spread, and a wide band lying at nearly one depth does not. What it follows from is how often the width turns over, which is the folding of (a); so of the geometry checks in (a), that is the one to read beside this distribution.
- The camera's tilt changes with the time-of-day mood; the night mood tilts it by a clearly visible amount (version 17: at night the view is flatter: the camera looks up at the sheet at a clearly smaller angle). (Version 22: fit the night placement and aim from night footage, by the same measurements, rather than scaling the daytime ones by eye.)

**e. The drive in plain terms (version 5).**

- The shaken end is the one at the left of the screen, where the motion enters; that is why the swell travels left to right (4.6). The large-scale swell of (b) runs the same way.
- Every row's end is shaken, not one corner. All the shaken ends follow the one shared path of (a), so neighbouring rows' ends are close together and move nearly in step, but across the whole edge the timing offset adds up to more than one full cycle, so the ends are strung out along that path rather than lying in one flat line (corrected in version 5.1, clarified in version 9).
- Nothing else is driven. The rest of the sheet only follows through the springs, apart from the tiny random nudges (a) and the separate slow bend (b).
- **No pull back to a rest shape (version 7).** Nothing pulls points towards a stored or flat rest position. The only influences on the net are those in (a): the springs, the damping, the small random nudges, the steady push at the free end and the shaken edge. The sheet's shape comes entirely from how these balance. (Version 18: this covers the envelope of (a) as well, which scales the shape the net has reached and never moves a point towards a rest position.) (Version 26: this is also what keeps the width's folds: see "Folded slack across its width" in (a). Anything that pulls the net towards a plane - a rest-shape force, a blend towards a regular grid, or across-width springs strong enough to haul the width straight - flattens the sheet into a strip.)
- **One plain oscillation per axis (version 7).** Each point of the shaken edge moves up and down by one smooth oscillation, and towards and away by another (the two frequencies of (a)). Nothing is layered on top: no extra shaking patterns, harmonics or secondary drives. (Version 30: the one exception is not an exception to the drive but to the clock it reads - see "The clock does not run for ever" in (b), where the warp's restart sweeps the driven edge rapidly along this same path for a fraction of a second.)
- **Why the folds cross (version 8).** Across the shaken edge, the towards-and-away timing offset builds up somewhat more than the up-and-down offset. That difference between the two axes is the source of the crossing folds.
- **Push and nudges always on (version 7).** The steady push along the length and the per-step random nudges are present at every step and are part of the look rather than a correction. Do not switch them off or fade them out.
- **Balance (version 13).** The look comes from a lightly damped sheet with small nudges. Do not reach it with heavy damping and strong nudges, or with a fast, large drive: that balance can match still statistics but trades one measure against another whenever it is re-tuned, and the sheet changes from second to second unlike the original.
- **How strong the nudges are (version 16).** A check that does not depend on the look: hold the shaken edge still and let the nudges alone act on the net until its motion has settled; the typical speed of the points in the free half of the sheet is then of the same order as the shaken edge's peak speed when it moves, give or take a factor of two, not a small fraction of it. A net whose nudge-driven motion is a small fraction of the drive's shows too few partings and crossings and never pinches. The stipple that strong nudges produced in earlier builds came from angle-only shading with a hard edge-on line; the shading of 4.5 (version 16) is made to carry them.
- **A slow, moderate drive (version 14; its comparison with the swell withdrawn in version 18).** The shaken edge moves smoothly and unhurriedly. Each end moves less than one grid spacing either way. Its up-and-down travel is clearly larger than its towards-and-away travel, and the towards-and-away motion is the faster of the two. Version 14 also said that one full swing of either of its two motions takes a few times longer than the swell takes to cross the screen: **that is wrong about the original and is withdrawn.** Nothing on screen shows the shaken edge's own rate: that end is off screen, and the timescales the band's picture shows are set mainly by the warp (4.4b), so do not read the drive's rate off those either. Set it from the relative statements here and in (a): light damping that outlasts the slower of its two motions, nudges of the same order as its peak speed, and the smooth, unhurried motion described in this bullet. (Version 26: nor is the drive where the band's substance comes from. A band that is too thin or too even wants the folded width of (a); a larger or faster shaken edge, or a smaller sheet under the same drive, buys a busier band at the price of the surface's own speed on screen and of the sparkles that ride on it, both of which footage pins.)
- The motion is smooth and continuous, with no bursts, pauses or jerks; the shaken edge traces slow, rounded paths that never exactly repeat.
- Fine detail (where lines meet, small wrinkles) re-forms within seconds while the swell takes roughly 10-15 s to cross the screen; both are always present at once.

### 4.5 Construction: shading, anti-aliasing and compositing

**Which direction.** The sheet's shading is built from a periodic thread pattern across the ribbon. The pattern is a function of the across-ribbon surface coordinate, so its iso-lines (the threads) run **along the length**. The original has a thread-width control and a profile that is pre-filtered by on-screen density (below). In the settings it actually uses, the threads are at full width and fuse into a continuous sheen. What reads as "threads" is the soft streaking produced by the angle-dependent shading of a rippled sheet and by overlapping layers. Recommended: make the continuous, angle-shaded sheet right first. Add a thread texture along the length only if it stays subtle and never shows as distinct lines at normal resolutions.

**Brightness and view angle (version 16; replaces the version-3 description).** The original shades **per vertex** of the surface mesh, in the projected space of 4.4c, and interpolates the results across each triangle; the two lookups below are then applied **per pixel** to the interpolated values. Per vertex it computes:

- the **cosine** between the surface normal and the direction from the camera to the point, both in the projected space. The normal is the cross product of the projected surface's two derivatives, left unnormalised, so its length is the area of the surface element in that space.
- a **size factor**: that normal's component along the line of sight (area times cosine), divided by the **distance** to the point, and scaled by a per-mood constant. Note the first power of the distance, not the square: this is not the true on-screen area. Relative to its screen size, a farther bit of the sheet keeps more light per pixel than a nearer one.

Because the projection stretches the vertical axis by the aspect ratio relative to the horizontal, this cosine is a distorted view angle: a face tilted about a horizontal axis reads as markedly more face-on than it truly is, while one tilted about a vertical axis reads nearly true. The edge-on glow is therefore reached only when the sheet is quite close to edge-on about the horizontal axis. Reproduce this by evaluating the surface, its normal and the angle after projection (4.4c).

The light of a bit of sheet is the sum of two terms, each multiplied by the size factor and by the edge fade:

- a **sheen term**: the base brightness times the pre-filtered thread profile. It has **no response of its own in the view angle**; the angle enters only through the size factor. The profile is chosen by the size factor, like a mip level. In the settings used the threads are fused ("Which direction" above), so the profile is flat over small and middling size factors, and **it falls away where the size factor is large**. The sheen therefore rises with the size factor from zero, is strongest at middling values, and is cut back for bits of sheet that appear large on screen (near, face-on, big cells). **Version 36 corrects what version 17 said next** (that those bits get their light from the second term alone, which is why the faces are faint and see-through): only the very largest size factors lose the sheen altogether, and over the faces between the folds as a whole enough of it survives that the sheen, and not the second term, is most of their light - see "What the shading does and does not decide" below. The faces are faint and see-through because the sheen is thinned there, and because the second term is nearly spent at those angles; not because the sheen is gone. **Its cut-off (version 17):** the profile stays flat until well past the size factor at which the Fresnel-like peak sits (both in the same units of the size factor), then falls linearly to nearly nothing, and fades out slowly after that.
- a **Fresnel-like term**: a strength times a response looked up with the **product** of the cosine and the size factor, not with the cosine alone. The response is one narrow peak a little way above zero in that product: nothing at exactly zero (exactly edge-on), a steep rise to the peak, and a fall to nothing from moderate values upwards. Because the index is a product, the peak sits at a smaller cosine (closer to edge-on) where the size factor is larger, and moves towards face-on where the size factor is small (far, foreshortened or small cells). This term is the soft bright strip just inside every edge-on turn. **Its shape (version 17):** it is exactly zero over the lower part of the range below the peak, rises steeply to the peak, and falls more gradually above it, in a long, thin tail. It is narrow: its width at half height is less than the peak's own distance from zero. A symmetric bump with no zero region is too wide.

The mood (section 7) changes the base brightness, the strength of the Fresnel-like term and the per-mood scale of the size factor. **It changes the balance, not only the level (version 17):** by day the Fresnel-like term is clearly the stronger; at night the two are close; at dawn and dusk the sheen is slightly the stronger. The size factor's scale is slightly lower at night and dusk than by day and at dawn.

**Where the sheet sits on these curves (version 28; replaces version 17's form of this check).** Version 17 said that over the sheet the product of the cosine and the size factor typically lands near the Fresnel-like peak, not far below it, and that a sizeable part of the sheet lies beyond the start of the sheen's cut-off. Both are true of the original, and neither is demanding enough to be worth checking: a build whose sheet sits well down both curves, with a sheen its cut-off barely touches, passes that test comfortably and has then been told by this document that its shading is not at fault. Say it the strong way instead, and say it in a form that does not depend on how wide your own two curves happen to be - a position measured in peak units is not the same quantity in two builds whose responses have different widths, so compare each build against the features of its own curves.

Over the sheet, with each bit weighted by how much of the screen it covers:

- the typical sample lies **above** the Fresnel-like peak, on its falling side. Not at the peak, and not below it.
- **more** of the sheet lies beyond the point on the response's falling side at which it has dropped to half its peak height than lies below the matching point on its rising side. Both of those points exist for any curve of the shape described above, whether or not your own tail ever reaches exactly zero, so this is a comparison you can always make. The original's sheet is piled up high on the falling side; a build whose sheet sits too low has it piled up on the rising side and below, and that comparison is the quickest way to see which side of the original you are on.
- the sheen's cut-off **bites**: taken over the sheet it removes a substantial share of the sheen's light, not a trace of it. A build whose cut-off removes next to nothing has its sheet below the cut-off's start, whatever shape it gave the profile, and it will get the uncut look - a band that reads as one fused slab - from that alone.

Check all three on your own geometry with your own curves, as in 4.4d, and check them mood by mood. In the original the three come out much the same in all four moods, varying less between moods than between moments of a single mood; so a build whose figures differ sharply from mood to mood has a per-mood difference the original does not have, and it is more likely to be in the camera or in the sheet (4.1 and 4.4) than here.

**Where a per-mood difference in those figures can and cannot come from (version 36).** All three depend on the geometry and on the per-mood scale of the size factor, and on nothing else. The per-mood **balance** of the two terms - the loudest of the mood's shading knobs - cannot move any of them at all, because it multiplies each term's light without moving where a bit of sheet sits on either curve. So if your figures differ sharply between moods, the per-mood size scale and anything per-mood in your camera are where to look, and the balance is not. In the original the scale is nearly the same in all four moods, and the one per-mood difference in its camera moves these figures the same way the scale does, which is why the spread between its moods stays as small as it is. One caution on reading these figures at all: they wobble from one moment to the next by about as much as the whole spread between the original's moods, so a figure taken from a single clip is worth much less than it looks, and a difference of that size between two of your own moods is not worth chasing.

A single response of the view angle times a size factor cannot reproduce any of this: it has no cut-off of the sheen, no shift of the glow with size, and no first-power distance term.

**Three warnings that travel with that check (version 28).** They matter more than the check does.

- **It is a check first and a lever second (corrected in version 36).** Version 28 said it was no lever at all: that moving the sheet along these curves cannot mend a band that reads as one slab. Sliding the sheet along them does move the face level, and in the same direction as the shape of the sheen's cut-off does - but it costs more in the picture outside the band than the cut-off does, and it widens the typical bright line, so of the two it is the second choice. "What the shading does and does not decide" below puts the mechanisms on one footing. What remains true is that the placement pulls the band's structure in opposite directions in different moods, so there is no one placement that mends it everywhere.
- **The faint light outside the band follows the placement harder than anything inside it does.** Move the sheet up the curves and change nothing else and much of the faint skirt around the band goes with it. So a build that matches the original's skirt and the band's brightest level today is not thereby placed right - the two can be satisfied together from more than one place, because the sheet's own shape feeds the skirt as well - and a build that moves to satisfy this check must expect to lose skirt and to have to find it again from the sheet's shape rather than from the shading.
- **One scale cannot do it.** Where the sheet sits and how wide your own two curves are make one question, not two. Scaling the size factor slides the sheet along both curves at once and scales the light as well, so in general there is no scale at which the Fresnel-like term's reach over the sheet and the share of the sheen that survives both come out right. If you cannot satisfy the three points at any scale, it is the shapes of your own curves that have to change - how far the response reaches above its peak, and where the cut-off starts relative to that peak - and not the scale.

**What the shading does and does not decide (version 36; replaces version 24's paragraph of this name and version 28's "That trade, priced").** Here the light of a bit of sheet depends on its tilt, its apparent size and its distance, and on nothing else. Version 24 drew from that the conclusion that the sheet which fills the band's faces and the sheet which lies above and below the band are, by those three, much the same thing, so that no shape of the two responses can tell them apart and every change that puts light on the faces puts more light outside the band than it puts on the faces. That conclusion is wrong, and it has cost two rounds. **The two terms do not land in the same places.** The sheen term is what fills the faces, and it is also very nearly all of the faint light just outside the band; the Fresnel-like term is concentrated in the folds. That holds in every mood. And the sheen's cut-off acts on the **size factor**, which is exactly what distinguishes a near, face-on bit of sheet from a far or foreshortened one - so where that cut-off begins, and how long its fall is, is a lever on the face level, and a strong one: across the range of shapes that keep the rest of this section's description true it moves the face level over the whole of what the original shows in any mood, and past it in both directions.

**One sentence of this section is easy to misread, and the misreading builds the wrong band.** "By day the Fresnel-like term is clearly the stronger" is a statement about the per-mood strengths, not about where the light ends up. The Fresnel-like response is narrow, so only the bits of sheet near its peak collect anything from it, while the sheen's profile covers most of the sheet: in every mood, the day mood included, **most of the sheet's light is the sheen's**. A build that reads that sentence as "by day most of the band's light is the Fresnel-like term" will build a band of separate strands with the background showing between them - empty faces, a huge fold-to-face ratio, too many partings and too many peaks in a column. That is a recognisable picture: it is what the Fresnel-like term alone looks like with the band's peak made to match.

**Three mechanisms can put light on the faces, and they are not equivalent.** They are the shape of the sheen's cut-off; the per-mood scale of the size factor, which slides the sheet along both curves; and the per-mood balance of the two terms. Per unit of face **level** - the measure a build with empty faces is failing, a face's light as a share of its own column's brightest point (4.6) - the cut-off is the strongest and the cheapest of the three in the picture just outside the band, and it is the only one of the three that leaves the typical bright line's width alone. The balance is the dearest in the band's structure: it widens the typical line markedly and it removes partings fastest. Scaling the size factor lies between the two on the band's structure, and it is the dearest of the three in the picture just outside the band.

Measured per unit of face **light** rather than face level that ordering reverses, and the reversal has a cause worth knowing, because it decides which of the two readings applies to you. A gain-like move - the balance, or either strength on its own - only scales light that is already on the screen, so what it spends outside the band is fixed by the ratio that is already there and cannot be anything else; but it lifts the folds as much as the faces, so it buys almost no face level. The cut-off instead adds light where the uncut sheen's light would lie, which is the big, near, face-on sheet: the faces, and the wings just above and below the band. So it buys face level cheaply and light outside the band dearly. If your folds are right and your faces are empty, yours is a face-level fault and the first ordering is the one that applies to it.

**What the trade really is.** None of the three buys face light without moving the band back towards one slab: each of them thickens it, lengthens the rise of its outer edges and removes partings, in much that pattern. That part of version 24 stands, and a build that has just won its band's structure out of these curves can give it back by moving them too far. But the cut-off does the least of that per unit of face level, so it is the mildest of the three rather than, as version 24 implied, out of the question. And the cost outside the band is only a cost where your own light outside the band already matches the footage's. Measure that mood by mood before you move anything: a build can be over on it in one mood and under in another, and then the same move is right in one and wrong in the other. Where you are **under** it in the same mood whose faces are empty, the cure and the cost point the same way and there is nothing to trade at all.

**The curve is shared and the moods are not.** The sheen's profile is one curve that every mood reads. What makes the original's cut-off bite differently in different moods is only that its sheet sits at a slightly different place on that one curve in each, through the per-mood scale of the size factor. So a change to the shape of the cut-off changes every mood at once, and the moods will not all want the same change: a build whose faces are empty by day may have a night band that is already right on its face level and its fold-to-face ratio, and too thick and too broad besides, and for that band the same move is wrong on most of the measures it is right on by day. Make the change on the curve, then re-check the per-mood size scale and the placement check above, mood by mood, and expect to have to move the scale.

**And one thing that looks like the sheet's fault and is not.** Where layers of sheet lie over one another their light adds, so it is natural to read empty faces as too few layers crossing over them. In the original the layering does the opposite: the layers pile up in the folds rather than on the faces, and each layer also puts down more light in a fold than on a face, so the layering **adds** to the fold-to-face contrast instead of relieving it. If every pixel's light were divided by the number of layers lying over it, the original's band would read **more** filled, not less. So a build whose faces are empty cannot explain them by its sheet carrying too few layers over the faces, and a build whose fold-to-face ratio is many times the original's has a fault far larger than anything the layering contributes.

**Two particular things that still will not do it.** The length of the tail above the Fresnel-like peak moves the band's line widths the wrong way, and changes nothing outside the band that a measure of the final frame can see. And how finely the surface is sampled across the ribbon's width hardly moves the band's widths at all, and moves them the wrong way: coarser sampling across widens the typical line rather than narrowing it. Raise that sampling density once as a convergence check and leave it alone.

**Nor does the drawing or the accumulation (version 27).** The paragraphs above say what the shape of the two responses can and cannot do. Two more things are ruled out, so that nobody spends a round on them. The first is how the sheet's light is accumulated. The buffer holds the accumulated light with ample precision for what the band actually stacks, and whether its range is bounded or unbounded makes a difference to the finished picture smaller than the dither moves it: the band's line widths, the width of the brightest line, the breadth of the sheen, the fold-to-face ratio, the fill and the peaks per column all stay where they were. The second is how the supersamples are weighted when the composite reads that buffer: a plain average over the block and a weighting that leans on one sample of it differ in grain, not in the width or the separation of anything. Neither decides whether the band reads as one fused tube or as distinct ribbons.

**Which fault points where (version 36; replaces the paragraph version 24 drew from the conclusion it has now lost).** In the original the faces between the folds are see-through, dark eye-shaped partings open between neighbouring strands, and the band's average light stays well under its peak - and the faces nevertheless carry a veil of light rather than nothing at all. The measures that show that balance are the band's fill, its face level, its fold-to-face ratio and the number and length of its partings, all taken with the band's peak matched and kept apart by mood. Read them together, because they tell apart three faults that want different cures.

- **The folds are at the right level and the faces are empty**, with a fold-to-face ratio far above the original's, more partings than it has and more peaks in a column. The sheen is not reaching the near, face-on sheet. The mechanisms priced above are the cure, the shape of the sheen's cut-off first; and if the faint light just outside your band is short in the same mood, the move costs you nothing there.
- **The faces are too full and the band reads as one slab**, with a shallow trough between neighbouring strands, few partings and an average light close to its peak. That is the same lever the other way: a cut-off whose fall begins earlier or runs steeper empties the faces, opens the partings and deepens the trough. It will take faint light from just outside the band with it, so measure that first - this is the case where the trade above is a real cost.
- **The folds themselves are wrong** - where they lie, how often they cross, how long a crossing runs - while your face level and your light outside the band already match the footage's. Then the quarrel is not about how the light is shared between faces and folds, and nothing in the shape of these two responses will settle it: what is left is how much sheet lies in each pixel and at what angle, which is how steeply and how often the sheet folds, how many layers lie over one another and at what angles, and the camera. That belongs to 4.1 and 4.4 rather than to this section, and expect to move it from the sheet's side.

**Two ceilings worth checking (version 36; replaces the single check of version 27).** Version 27 told you to render the wave alone, with nothing under it, and to see that its brightest place falls well short of the top of the output range in every mood. Keep that check and keep its reasoning: a build whose folds reach the top of the range on their own has too much light in its brightest folds however right its band's median level is, and it clips detail out of exactly the places where the original keeps its crispest lines. But it is not enough on its own, and a build can pass it with room to spare while the screen still blows out - because what a viewer sees is the finished frame, and there the band rides on the background beneath it. So check the finished frame as well, background and wave and glare and sparkles together as the screen gets it, **in every mood and in every month** rather than only the months you have footage of; count the pixels that reach the top level, and look at where they lie and what is under them.

What to expect of the original: version 27 said the only pixels that reach the top level are inside the band at dusk, and that is wrong in both directions. Pixels at the top level turn up at every mood hold and in every month - a bare handful at night, more in the daylight moods - and in some months at dusk and at dawn the crest of the band, which tops out only with its own glare added on top of it, clips over a connected patch. So the test is not that nothing may clip, nor even that no patch may clip. It is **what lies underneath the clipped pixels**, and there the original is strict: the brightest things in its frame are the cores of sparkles and the crests of the band, and **the background on its own never reaches the top level, in any mood or any month**. So sort your own clipped pixels by what they need - drop the sparkles, drop the wave, drop the glare layer in turn, and see which of them each pixel depended on. Clipped sparkle cores and a clipped band crest are in the original's character. A patch that still clips with the wave and the sparkles taken away, or one that needs only the glare lying over a bright background with no fold under it, is not: that is a background too bright for its mood or a bright-pass being fed too much (section 5), and it is to be cured there and not by dimming the wave.

The two checks fail in different ways and want different cures. Folds that reach the ceiling on their own are the wave's own brightness, and the per-mood wave brightness below is the thing to move. A frame that tops out while the wave alone is far from the ceiling is the band riding on a bright background, or the glare sitting on top of that, and then the place to look is the background's own level in that mood and month, and what is being fed to the bright-pass (section 5) - not the wave's brightness, which if you lower it to stop the clipping will take that mood's whole band level down with it. Either way, the observable described below stops telling the truth where the band clips, so run this check before you trust a fit made with it.

**The projected-size scaling is required (version 10).** It is not only a guard against slivers:
it applies to every bit of the sheet at every size, and it shapes the look.

- A bit of sheet spread over many pixels gives more light per pixel than the same bit squeezed into
  a thin sliver. The light grows steadily with the apparent size (version 16: up to middling size
  factors; beyond them the sheen term is cut back, see above - **version 36:** thinned rather than
  removed, so that near, face-on sheet still gets most of its light from the sheen, and only the
  very largest size factors lose it altogether).
- Exactly at the silhouette, where the sheet turns edge-on, the light therefore falls towards
  nothing. There is no separate hard line there. A fold's glow sits just inside the turn, where the
  sheet is still visibly tilted, and is spread over a soft strip several pixels wide.
- Brightness from the view angle alone, peaking where the sheet is edge-on, gives the wrong look:
  thin hard lines, dark bands between them, and a picture that churns from one second to the next.
- On screen, the scaling makes the bright lines wider and softer, lights more of the band and keeps
  the picture steadier. It also tends to reduce the number of separate lines and partings and to add
  some pinching, so re-tune with it in place.
- **Area included (version 10.1).** The apparent size includes how much of the cloth each bit holds, not only its tilt and its distance: the surface element's area (as defined under "Brightness and view angle" above) is part of the factor. Where the sheet is bunched or compressed, each bit covers less of the screen and gives less light. The cosine and the distance alone leave this out, and give thinner lines, less of the band lit and a less steady picture.

**Anti-aliasing.** The sheet is drawn into its own **off-screen buffer at a higher resolution than the screen** (supersampling). The buffer holds a single intensity and is accumulated with additive blending, at enough precision that many overlapping faint layers add up without banding or clipping. When the composite reads the buffer, it filters it down with a small box filter. Supersampling plus filtering is the requirement; the factor is yours. **The filter is one output pixel wide (version 16):** it covers exactly the supersample block of one output pixel, no more. The softness of the original's lines comes from the shading above, not from this filter; do not widen the filter to soften lines. (Version 17: the original also does not widen cells thinner than a pixel to a minimum width: a sliver lights only the supersamples it covers, and with the size factor its light is faint anyway.) **Version 27:** none of the choices in this paragraph decides the band's structure, as "Nor does the drawing or the accumulation" above says. Make them reasonable and then leave them alone.

**Compositing (version 17: the tints and the exposure stated).** In one full-screen pass the original, in order:

- first, upscales the background gradient smoothly. **Version 24:** version 17 said its tint down the screen is plain white, that is, no tint; withdraw that claim. Nothing on screen can separate a tint applied here from the background's own colours, because both act before the exposure and tone curve, so this document no longer says which carries the profile: keep the whole top-to-bottom profile of the background in the background's own colours, and where a smooth top-to-bottom level error survives against footage, correct it there and not here.
- next, adds the filtered wave intensity times a neutral (grey, never coloured) tint that brightens slightly from left to right.
- last, passes the sum, background and wave together, through the mood's **exposure and tone curve**, and writes the result.

**How far that tint brightens across the screen (version 20).** The ramp is slight, and this document does not fix how slight: measure it rather than choosing it. On footage, compare the brightness of the band's brightest places in the left part of the picture with the same thing in the right part, averaged over several seconds and over several conditions, so that the band's own wandering, tilting and arching average out, and set the ramp to match what you measure. Do not judge it by eye on a single frame: one frame says more about where the folds happen to lie than about the ramp.

**A sharper form of that measurement (version 27).** A ratio of two numbers taken from the band's brightest places is noisier than the thing it is meant to measure: it varies from clip to clip of the original by more than the error you are hunting, and clips of the same conditions disagree about it by more again, so a build can sit comfortably inside that spread with a ramp of visibly the wrong steepness. Take instead the band's added light - the band with the background beneath it fitted and subtracted - as a **profile across the whole width of the picture**, averaged over every frame, every second and every condition you have, and fit the ramp to the slope of that profile. A profile uses every column rather than the few that happen to hold the brightest folds, and pooling everything available is what lifts the slope above the band's own wandering. Work out the uncertainty of your own fitted slope before believing any difference you see in it. **Version 28:** this fit is made against your own sheet, so it goes stale whenever the sheet changes. Re-run it after any change to the sheet's shape, and do not carry a ramp fitted on one sheet over to another.

**The exposure and tone curve (version 17).** It is applied to the whole composite, not only to the glare:

- first, a small black offset is subtracted: the darkest part of the range goes to black, and everything else is lowered by as much. **Version 20:** the offset is the same in every mood, and the mood changes only the gain and the white point below it, never where the curve reaches zero.
- then a gain that depends on the mood: lowest by day, slightly higher at dawn, higher again at dusk, and highest at night.
- then the textbook soft tone curve with a white point (the extended Reinhard curve, as in the glare's bright-pass, section 5). By day the white point is set so that the curve lifts the brightest values slightly; in the other moods it is neutral.

**The dawn gain's place in that order is not negotiable (version 27).** "Slightly higher at dawn" is meant as written, and the original really is built that way: the dawn gain sits just above the day gain. A joint fit will want to push it much further, because the whole dawn picture of a build whose dawn background is too dark can be lifted that way. Do not loosen the order to let it through. At the dawn hold the mood is a pure dawn, so a dawn picture that is too dark overall, or whose lower half is, points at the dawn background's own colours (sections 3 and 7); a dawn band that is flat rather than dim points at the shading's balance for that mood (above). Neither points at the gain, which moves the background and the band together.

**The gain and the mood's own wave brightness are two different things (version 28).** The band's level in a mood is the product of both, and this document keeps them apart because the screen does. The **gain** multiplies the background and the wave together, just before the tone curve. The mood's **wave brightness** - the per-mood scales on the two shading terms described above - multiplies the wave's light and nothing else. Either will brighten a band; only one of them brightens the background with it, and that is what tells them apart on footage.

**The observable that separates them (version 28).** Take the band's added light - the band with the background beneath it fitted and subtracted, the same separation the tint's ramp and the night band's colour already use - integrate it down each column over the band's extent, and divide by the level of that same fitted background at that column. Average over many frames and over every condition of that mood you have, and set your own per-mood wave brightness so that your renders give what footage of that mood gives. The gain multiplies numerator and denominator alike, so it very nearly cancels; the wave's own brightness multiplies the numerator alone. In the moods whose white point is neutral the cancellation is almost exact over a wide range of gain, and in the day mood, whose white point is not neutral, it is looser but still far tighter than the thing being measured.

Five things to get right. Each of them will otherwise cost you a round.

- **Divide by the background under the band, not beside it - and know what that denominator brings with it (extended in version 36).** At night the picture beside the band is at the floor, so a ratio against it is not a measurement at all, and in some columns it divides by nothing. The right denominator is the per-column fit from the rows above and below the band, evaluated at the band's own row - which your background removal already computes. But that denominator carries the background's own top-to-bottom gradient, so the ratio inherits **where your band happens to sit**: two builds with equally bright bands, one of them sitting a little lower in the frame than the other, give different ratios, and the steeper the background's gradient under the band the larger the difference. It is worst in the moods whose background changes fastest down the screen, and there the ratio moves more with the band's own wandering than with the thing you are trying to set. So before using it, compare your own fitted background under the band with the footage's at the same condition. Where the two agree, use the ratio. Where they do not, it is not a measurement of your band's brightness at all: fit on the band's **added light** instead, and report the difference in the background under the band beside the fit, so that whoever reads it knows which of the two it rests on.
- **Integrate down the column; do not take the column's brightest pixel.** The brightest pixel in a column is often a sparkle; the sparkles' light is added after the curve and does not follow the gain; so the peak form drifts with the gain by enough to hide the very separation you are using. The column integral is the steadiest form once the sparkles are in the picture, and the mean over the band's extent is close behind.
- **Check that the band's brightest places are still inside the output range** at the condition you fit on. The ratio tells the truth until the top of the band clips, and then it collapses. It is the ceiling that ends the observable, not the bend in the tone curve.
- **It is a per-mood target against footage of that mood, and never a comparison between moods.** The background's own level differs enormously between night and noon, so the ratio is not the same quantity in two moods and its size says nothing when carried across them.
- **It sets the band's level in a mood, not the band's structure.** If your band reads as one fused tube where the original's is separate ribbons, moving the per-mood wave brightness moves the whole band's level and leaves the fill, the face level, the fold-to-face ratio and the partings very nearly where they were. "What the shading does and does not decide" above says which mechanisms do move it; do not spend this value on it.

**And this fit goes stale (version 36).** Like the tint's ramp above, it is made against your own sheet - and against where that sheet puts the band on the screen, which the denominator above makes it sensitive to. So re-run it after any change to the sheet's shape or to the camera, and do not carry a per-mood wave brightness fitted on one sheet over to another. The same holds for everything else in this section that is fitted against the band.

**And the stretch of footage it is measured over matters (version 36).** The band's light is not the same at every moment. It varies continuously with the state of the cloth - the band breathes, brightening and dimming as the sheet folds and unfolds, and its thickness moves with its brightness because both follow the same pose - and over a short clip that variation is as large as the differences this observable is used to settle. It is not a settling-in either: it does not grow or fade with the time since the start, so do not look for a run-up over the first minutes and do not write one into a build. What follows is how the figure must be taken: as an average over a stretch long enough that it stops depending on **where the stretch starts**. How long that is, is to be established rather than assumed - take the longest footage you have of one condition, compute the figure over every window of a given length, and lengthen the window until the spread between windows is small beside the difference you are trying to measure. A single short clip will not do, however many frames you average inside it; and neither will many clips that all begin at the same time since the start, because those are all the same moment of the same cloth. Match conditions between footage and your own renders - the same mood, the same month, the same time since the start - and if your figure disagrees with the footage's by a different amount at different moments, do not try to mend that with this value: a band that breathes differently from the original's is the sheet's motion (4.1 and 4.4) showing up in a look measurement, and fitting the brightness to one moment would bake the error in.

**The two orderings run against each other (version 28).** The gains and the wave's own brightness are not merely separate: in the original they are ordered differently, and a build that misses this cannot be rescued by its gains. The gains are as above - lowest by day, slightly higher at dawn, higher again at dusk, highest at night. **The wave's own brightness is lowest by day, a little above that at night, clearly higher at dusk, and highest of all at dawn.** The mood with the highest gain therefore has nearly the lowest wave brightness, and the mood with nearly the lowest gain has the highest. The two very nearly cancel: in the original the band's own added light in the finished frame comes out much the same in the three dark moods and clearly lower by day. That is the band's added light itself, in the output's own levels - **not** the ratio defined above, which is not comparable between moods and must not be made equal across them.

**And that is where the push on the dawn gain comes from (version 28).** A build whose per-mood wave brightness is flat - the same in every mood, or near enough - cannot match the band in four moods with gains in the order above and properly spaced apart. Fit four gains to four moods' bands with a flat wave brightness and they come out in the right order but squeezed together, the night and dusk gains nearly equal and **the dawn gain pushed far above the day gain**: exactly the push the paragraph above tells you to refuse. The order is not the real constraint in that fit; the flat wave brightness is. So if you cannot match the band in every mood while keeping the gains in the order above and well spaced, do not loosen the order and do not blame the fit. Move the per-mood wave brightness, measured as this section says, and let the gains settle after it. A build that is too dim in one mood and too bright in another, with the shape of its band right in the dim one, has this fault and not a shading fault.

**How far the floor reaches, and how to find it (version 24; replaces the paragraph of version 20).** This document does not fix how much is subtracted: measure it. Two things on screen pin it down, and the sharper of the two is a colour rather than a depth.

*The colour the band's light takes at night.* The same amount is taken from every channel and nothing may fall below zero, so wherever the background is dark its weaker channels are cut away entirely while its strongest channel survives. Light added over such a place can only show in the channels whose total clears the floor, so over the dark parts of a night frame the band's own light takes on the colour of the background it lies over: in a month whose night background is strongly coloured the band's added light carries that colour, and in a month whose night background is near-neutral it stays neutral. Take the floor down and all three channels pass, and the added light reads grey in every month. To use this, separate the band's added light from the background on night footage - fit and subtract the background beneath the band, or difference against a moment when the band lies elsewhere - look at the balance of the three channels in what is left, month by month, and fit your offset so that your own renders show the same balance. **Version 27 replaces what version 24 said next about this observable** (that it answers steadily across the whole useful range and goes blind at neither end): that is true only in the form described below, and false in the form it is most naturally measured in. Two cautions from version 24 still hold. How strongly the colour shows depends on the night palettes as well (sections 3 and 7), so some residual may remain once the offset is right; but a night band that reads neutral grey in a month whose background is strongly coloured cannot be explained by the palettes alone. And by day nothing in the frame is near the floor, so nothing is cut away and the band's added light should read near-neutral whatever colour the background is: if yours is neutral at night and leans away from the background's colour by day, something in your curve is acting unevenly between the channels, because the wave's own tint is grey (above) and cannot be the cause.

**What that observable really pins down (version 27).** Not the floor on its own. Taking the floor down by some amount, and darkening the whole night background by the same amount in every channel, are the same arithmetic: they produce the same frame, pixel for pixel, at every hour. So neither the floor nor the dark end of the night background is an observable quantity by itself; what is observable, and what this measurement pins, is **where the dark end of the night background sits relative to the point at which the curve reaches zero**. Fit that, and stop asking which of the two to blame. One consequence is cheering: if the dark end of your night pictures can be moved, you need never touch the floor at all - the same correction can be made entirely there - and the two routes cannot be told apart on the screen.

**And the form of the measurement decides whether it says anything at all (version 27).** Averaged over a clip it says nothing. A per-month, per-channel scaling of the night background can reproduce the three channel shares of the band's added light at almost any floor, because a month offers three free scalings against only two independent numbers in an average; and more months do not help, since each new month brings its own new scalings. What a scaling cannot do is serve several band positions at once: the band's dark pixels lie over a range of background levels, the wave sweeps them across that range, and a scaling can imitate a floor at only one level. So measure the shares **separately at several moments, with the band in clearly different places, and require every one of them to match**. Two well-separated positions carry nearly all of the information; the average of many carries none. In the original those shares do shift a little from one band position to the next in a strongly coloured month, and that shift is the signal rather than noise to be averaged away.

**Which months can speak, and when a month is silent (version 27).** A month carries information about this only where one channel of its night background, under the band, falls below the floor: there that channel is cut away, and how much is cut is what the floor sets. A month whose night background is near-neutral agrees with every value, and so does a month whose night background is bright enough that almost none of the band lies over anything dark - and that second case is the dangerous one, because the measure still returns a confident-looking number from a handful of pixels. Report the count of qualifying pixels - band pixels over dark background - beside every month's shares, treat a month with few of them as silent rather than as evidence, and do not let the silent ones vote. Check which of your months speak before you fit, and make sure at least two of the months you fit on do.

**Why a fit keeps preferring the floor it already has, and what to do about it (version 27).** A fit that may only *scale* the background's colours cannot follow a change in the floor. A scaling absorbs part of the floor's effect on the background's level and leaves a residue with a definite shape: too bright through the upper middle of the frame, where the original is still black, too dark along the bottom, and the flat black top too short. So a whole-frame comparison of the background will always prefer the floor the build already has, and a joint fit that lets that comparison decide will keep it there - however clearly the night band's colour says otherwise. That preference is a property of what the fit is allowed to do, not evidence about the floor. The remedy is not to overrule the colour: it is to let the dark end of the night pictures *move* and not only scale, which the description of those pictures in section 3 already allows - the restriction is in the fitting, not in the model. Once the fit can move it, the floor and that movement are the same knob, the conflict disappears, and the colour observable in the form above fixes the combination that matters. Until then, do not let a whole-frame background comparison choose the floor, and do not read a background that got worse after a floor correction as evidence that the floor was right before.

*The depth of the night black foot.* How far down a night frame the top of the picture stays at its darkest before the background's gradient lifts off it, as a fraction of the screen height, measured over several frames and over the night conditions available. Two warnings go with it. **The form of the measure matters more than the measure does.** Do not count pixels or rows sitting exactly at the darkest output level. The original's near-black top carries a fine dither between its lowest steps, and a delivery encode at a modest bitrate merges those steps, so on footage that has been through one a count of darkest-level pixels reads the foot far too deep and invents wandering between frames that the picture does not have. Instead smooth along the row, take a low percentile over the columns, and find the first row at which that profile stands a small amount above its own level at the very top of the frame - the smallest amount that clears the picture's own grain. In that form the depth agrees between a modest-bitrate delivery and a lossless one, and is steady enough between moments to fit on. **And the depth is a joint observable.** It depends on the offset and on how dark the top of your own night background is, so it pins the offset down only if it actually moves when the offset moves. Check that on your own build before you fit: take your offset over a wide range and see whether your foot's depth follows. If it hardly does, then your night background's own top, and not the offset, is what ends your flat region; the depth cannot fit the offset on your build, and the colour observable above has to carry the fit.

Further signs that the offset is too small: the top of the night picture is not quite black and the whole frame sits lifted; faint sheet light that the original crushes away survives, so the veiling around the band spreads wider and reads flatter than the original's own faint skirt (section 5); and the band's outer edges rise over a longer distance, most of all in the few places where that rise is longest. Use that last one to catch an offset that is badly wrong rather than to fine-tune, because it stops answering once the offset is anywhere near right. Signs that it is too large: the flat black foot reaches further down the frame than the footage shows, and faint sheet light the original keeps is lost. **Three things that look as though they should answer to the offset and do not (version 24).** The crispness of the picture just outside the band does not follow it at all, and in any case most of what a window there measures is the sparkles rather than the sheet. The crispness and sharpness inside the band follow it so weakly that the sparkles move those measures further than the whole useful range of the offset does. And how steady the foot's depth is from moment to moment is not an offset observable either: in the original that depth hardly moves between moments, and on a build where it wanders with the wave's state the cause is faint sheet light reaching into the dark top of the frame rather than the offset, so look for it in the sheet and not here. The band's own contrast barely moves with the offset as well: it changes what lies around the folds, not the folds themselves, so do not expect it to fix a band that is dim or flat.

**After a change to the offset (version 24).** The same amount is taken from every pixel, so the whole frame's levels move with it, and the moods' gains and the background's colours (sections 3 and 7) no longer fit what they were fitted against. Re-check them after any change to the offset, and report how far the frame moved rather than quietly absorbing it into them.

The curve is what makes the top of the night picture black and keeps faint parts of the wave over a dark background from showing. Design the background's colours to look right after it. The wave is near-neutral light, and its colour on screen comes from the background it is added to.

### 4.6 Measured look and motion (from rendered output)

Every number in 4.6 was measured from rendered frames of the original at 1920x1080 (wave alone, isolated from the background) and cross-checked against public footage. Heights (H) are fractions of the screen height measured from the top; widths (W) are fractions of the screen width; pixels are at 1920x1080. Ranges show how much the look varies between moments and times of day; they are not targets for one frame. Timescales are deliberately rounded.

**Overall band**

- The band's centre line sits a little below the middle of the screen on average: typically 0.45-0.70 H, median about 0.57 H. Its top edge rarely rises above about 0.3 H and its bottom edge rarely falls below about 0.86 H. Over several minutes the average height of the band wanders between roughly 0.45 and 0.70 H. At a single point along the screen the centre can range from about 0.3 to 0.8 H.
- Thickness (top to bottom of the visible band in one column) is typically 0.07-0.23 H, median about 0.15 H. Averaged over time it is even from left to right; at any moment the thickest part is about 1.5-4 times the thinnest. In about one column in ten the band pinches below 0.08 H, down to a thin bright line (under 0.05 H in a few percent of columns).
- The band tilts both ways. From one side of the screen to the other its centre typically changes by up to about a third of the screen height either way. It bends into an arch or a sag of up to about 0.2 H, or lies nearly straight. On screen the centre line usually shows a single gentle S or arch (its visible wavelength is about one screen width or more). The thickness swells and narrows on a shorter scale, about half a screen width.

**Strands and partings**

- Counting brightness peaks down a column, the band shows 3-4 peaks in a typical column. With prominence of at least 12 % of the column's brightest point, columns have 1 / 2 / 3 / 4 / 5 / 6 / 7+ peaks in about 3 / 15 / 29 / 29 / 16 / 6 / 2 % of cases. Counting only the stronger peaks (at least 25 % or 40 % of the column's brightest point) gives 2-3 and 1-2. The left third of the screen is a little simpler (about 3) than the middle and right (about 4).
- Between strands there are dark, eye-shaped partings: on average 1-2 per column. About 8-13 partings at least 5 % of the width long are visible at once, typically 3-14 % of the width long and rarely more than a third.
- Strands meet (cross or touch) where a parting closes. Counting every closing gives about 25-50 per screen width; counting only those of the larger partings gives about 15-30. They are spread across the screen, slightly fewer at the far right.
- Bright lines meet at shallow angles, typically a few degrees and rarely more than about 15 degrees. Two lines therefore run fused for a stretch of roughly 30-200 px before they separate again. Crossings read as long, slow X shapes, never sharp intersections.

**Brightness profile across the band**

- Faces seen face-on are faint. In a column the face level is about 0.12-0.30 of the column's brightest point (median 0.2).
- The brightest fold in a column is typically 3-8 times the face level (median about 5, up to about 12). An average peak of any kind is about 2-5 times the face level (median about 3).
- Overlaps: within one column the brightest face segment between peaks is typically 1.2-3 times the faintest (median about 1.8). Overlapping layers add visibly, but they do not jump to a separate, much brighter level.
- Brightness rises smoothly from a face towards a fold over tens of pixels and peaks at the line where the sheet is seen edge-on. There are no steps or plateaus.
- Line sharpness: bright lines have a full width at half maximum of about 4-9 px (median about 6 px; the brightest line in a column about 5-11 px, median about 8 px). They are soft, glowing lines, not 1-2 px hairlines. A typical brightest line stands out by about half of its own height above its surroundings.
- The outer edges of the band rise from 10 % to 90 % over about 1.5-9 px, median about 3.5 px. The edge is soft, never a hard cut.
- Broad sheen maxima inside the faces are about 8-37 px wide at half height (median about 19 px), roughly a tenth of the band's thickness.

**Streaks**

- What reads as streaks are thin bright lines running along the length of the band, plus broad, soft, lighter bands inside the faces, also along the length. Nothing runs across the band and no thread texture is visible.
- About 10-17 lines at least 10 % of the screen width long are visible at once. Of these, about 1-5 are at least a quarter of the width. Typical length is 12-28 % of the width, the longest about half.
- A line's peak is typically 1-2.5 times the band's median level in that column (median 1.5).
- Lines gather at the band's upper and lower fringes: the outer fifth on each side holds over half of them. They also gather where the band pinches. They are a little rarer in the left third of the screen.
- A given line stays recognisable for roughly 1-3 s while it moves. The arrangement of lines is reshuffled within a few seconds (roughly 3-6 s).

**Motion** (timescales rounded)

- The dominant visible motion is a slow **swell**: the band rises and falls, and that rise and fall travels along the band from left to right. It takes roughly 10-15 s to cross the screen and was seen in every sample. Up and down, the band's centre moves at typically 0.01-0.03 H per second (median about 0.02 H/s). Averaged over several seconds the whole band drifts more slowly, under 0.01 H/s.
- Within 40 s the band's average height typically changes by 0.1-0.25 H. The tilt and the arch swing back and forth on a timescale of a few tens of seconds. The overall height wanders more slowly, over minutes. Nothing repeats regularly.
- The strands move **together**. Adjacent bright lines differ in vertical speed by only a few pixels per second (median about 5 px/s), about a fifth of the band's common up-and-down motion. They slide past each other slowly, so a crossing drifts along gently.
- Visible features (crossings, line ends, bulges) move along the screen slowly, typically 10-40 px/s (about 0.5-2 % of the width per second). They move rightwards slightly more often than leftwards and often reverse.
- Crossings and partings persist from under a second to several seconds: a median of about 0.5-1 s, and the longest 6-9 s.
- How fast the picture changes: after about 1 s the band region is still clearly the same picture (about half its detail in place). After about 5 s it is mostly re-formed, mainly because the band has moved up or down and changed its folds.

**Coarse timescales and sizes** (summary, rounded on purpose)

| what a viewer sees | coarse value |
|---|---|
| a swell travelling across the visible band | roughly 10-15 s, left to right |
| visible wavelength along the band | centre line: about one screen width or more; thickness changes: about half a width |
| a visible disturbance (crossing, kink, line arrangement) fading or re-forming | about 1 s to a few seconds |
| dominant rhythms | the travelling swell; tilt and arch swinging over a few tens of seconds; slower wandering of the overall height over minutes |
| arch / sag of the centre line | up to about 0.2 H |
| tilt across the screen | up to about a third of the screen height either way |
| vertical drift of the band's centre | about 0.1-0.25 H within 40 s; about 0.3 H over minutes |
| busyness over a minute | steady and calm, with no bursts or pauses: always some swell, a few crossings drifting, line arrangement changing every few seconds; irregular, never periodic |

### 4.7 Character of the effect

**Motion character.** Heavy rather than light, lazy rather than restless. The sheet behaves like a long, soft cloth in a slow current. Folds form and unform gradually as the swell passes; nothing snaps, flips or jitters. The whole ribbon moves as one body: its strands rise and fall together and only slowly slide past each other. Small detail (where lines meet, where a fold pinches) keeps changing over seconds, while the large shape changes over tens of seconds.

**Brightness and viewing angle.** Brightness depends smoothly on how the sheet is seen. It is faint where the sheet faces the viewer, grows steadily as the sheet turns away, and is brightest along the line where it is seen exactly edge-on. That line is soft and glowing, not a hairline. The curve has no steps, bands or plateaus.

**See-through and additive.** The sheet is translucent everywhere. The background shows through every face, and where layers overlap their light adds. An overlap is brighter but still see-through, never opaque, and never hides a layer behind it.

**What is absent.**

- No visible texture or separate threads at normal resolutions; only smooth sheen and soft streaks along the length.
- No shadows: the sheet casts none on itself or on the background.
- No specular highlight from a light source; brightness comes from the viewing angle and the folds only.
- The wave does not light the background around it, beyond the soft glare halo described in section 5.
- No hard edges, outlines or saturated colour.

**What matters most to the look, ranked.**

1. One coherent, translucent ribbon that folds over itself: faint faces, soft bright edge-on folds, and overlaps that add.
2. The slow swell travelling left to right, with the strands moving together.
3. Placement and proportions: centre a little below the middle, about 0.15 H thick on average, even from left to right, pinching to a thin bright line in places, tilting and arching gently.
4. The fold-to-face contrast (folds about 5 times the face level) and soft, slightly wide glowing lines and edges.
5. Long, slow, shallow-angle crossings and eye-shaped partings between 2-4 strands.
6. Fine streaks along the length, gathered at the band's fringes and pinches.

## 5. Glare

### Look

- The brightest parts of the background-plus-wave image (mostly the densest folds of the wave on a bright daytime background) get a soft, wide, faint halo, never a visible blob.
- Glare strength follows the time of day. It is present in the daytime moods and **absent at night** (version 24: absent on the screen).
- **Which moods glare most, corrected (version 20).** Ordered by how much of the picture is bright enough to pass the bright-pass, and so by how much halo there is, the original glares most at dusk, less by day, least at dawn, and not at all at night. The earlier statement that it is strongest at dawn was wrong; this ordering holds in every month observed. **Version 24:** the night is free of halo on the screen. Keep it so; if a night frame of yours carries a halo a viewer could see, its threshold is too low or its folds are too bright. Do not reach a halo-free night by switching the bright-pass or the glare chain off in that mood: keep the same chain in every mood and let the night fall where it falls. **Version 28:** nor by putting the night's threshold out of reach. In the original the night's threshold is the **lowest** of the four, and the night is free of halo because the night band never comes near even that. A build whose night threshold is the highest of its four has reached the right answer by the wrong route, and it will stay right only by luck: it has no margin to report and nothing to notice if its night folds grow too bright.
- **Faint, but not nothing (version 20; qualified in version 28).** By day the halo reads as a slight softening of the band's surroundings rather than as a glow a viewer would point at. Version 20 went on to say that a build whose glare layer changes nothing whatever while the sun is up has something wrong in its bright-pass. **That is true of the daylight year taken together and false of some individual months.** How much glare there is swings enormously with the month and even between moments seconds apart at one condition: in the original it runs from a halo a careful viewer could see, in a month whose background sits near the threshold, down to nothing whatever - not a single pixel passing - in a month whose background sits far below it. So a daylight month with no glare at all is not by itself a fault, and must not be chased with the threshold. What *is* a fault, and a large one, is a glare that does **not** swing: a steady, broad glow of much the same size in every month and at every moment is wrong whatever its average, and no re-tuning of the strength will mend it.
- **A window outside the band does not measure the glare (version 20).** The picture just outside the band's edges carries faint light from the sheet itself: the wave's light does not stop where the band is usually taken to end, but continues outwards as a faint skirt, and farther out as occasional wide, very faint wisps of sheet. In a window from a few pixels to a few tens of pixels outside the band's edge, that sheet light is the larger part by far of what is there and the glare is the smaller part. A halo statistic over such a window is therefore mostly a measurement of the band's own outer light, and a build that falls short there falls short of sheet light, not of glare. Two consequences for tuning, both important. Do not raise the glare's strength to make such a statistic match: it would take a glare far stronger than the original's and the picture would go hazy. And measure your own glare instead by rendering the same frame with the glare layer switched on and off and differencing the two; that difference is your glare and nothing else.
- **The halo is local, and a broad lift is not a halo (version 27).** Nearly all of the light the glare adds lies close to the folds that passed the bright-pass; further out it falls away so steeply that over most of the screen it changes no pixel of the finished frame at all. The coarse levels of the pyramid below are broad in shape, but on their own they change nothing a viewer or a measure of the finished frame can see. Two things follow, and both matter for tuning. If your glare layer lifts a broad area of the picture, the cause is not the pyramid's weights, the blur radii or the glare's strength, and re-tuning those will only take the halo off the folds as well: the cause is that a broad part of your picture is passing your bright-pass, and what feeds the bright-pass is where to look. And a broad, very faint lift is not something to chase in the other direction either: it lies below the output's own step and below the dither's grain, so it cannot be measured on footage and no part of it should be fitted. In the original, away from the weeks named under the threshold below, the brightest part of the background by itself keeps real headroom under the bright-pass in every mood and month - narrowest by day in the brightest months - so nothing but the folds passes, and the folds cover a very small part of the screen.

### Construction (the textbook HDR bloom chain the original uses)

1. **Bright-pass** at reduced resolution, taken from the composited background and wave (not from the sparkles): apply an exposure and a soft tone curve with a white point, keeping only what exceeds it. **Version 17:** in the original the exposure and tone curve are the composite's own (4.5), and the bright-pass reads the composite after them. The bright-pass keeps the colour: it computes one weight per pixel from the brightest of the pixel's three channels (a soft knee: zero up to a threshold, rising above it) and multiplies the pixel's own colour by that weight. Nothing is desaturated or pulled towards white. The pyramid starts small: its first level is already far below the screen's resolution.
2. **Mip pyramid:** downsample repeatedly, halving the size each time, down to a very small level.
3. **Blur** each level with a **separable Gaussian** (horizontal, then vertical). The radius differs slightly between the colour channels, which gives the halo a faint colour fringe. (Version 17: the radius grows slightly from red to blue, blue the widest.)
4. **Weighted sum** of the blurred levels, coarsest to finest. The weights form a geometric series (each level a fixed ratio of the next), normalised to an overall glare strength. **Version 17:** the finer levels weigh more: each coarser level carries clearly less weight than the next finer one.
5. **Add** the result to the screen.

**How the levels are weighted, confirmed (version 27).** The ordering given for the weighted sum above is right: the finer levels weigh more, and by enough that the coarse levels contribute shape without contributing anything a viewer or a measure of the finished frame can see. Read "The halo is local" in the Look section before changing any of these weights.

**How big the chain is (version 28).** Version 17 said only that the chain's first level is already far below the screen's resolution. That is true, and it is not enough: the size of the chain is a property of the **picture**, not of the output's pixel count. The original's first level is a coarse grid laid over the whole picture - coarse enough that the finest detail the halo can carry is already a visible patch rather than a pixel - and it stays the same grid as the output grows, so the halo keeps the same width as a fraction of the picture. A chain built by dividing the output resolution by a fixed number of pixels does not behave that way: it gets finer as the output grows, so the halo's width as a fraction of the picture shrinks with every step up in size, and the same light arrives concentrated into a much higher peak over a smaller area - a tight bright rim on the folds where the original has a broad soft lift hugging the band.

Three things follow.

- **Make it a test.** Render the same moment at two clearly different output sizes, difference each against the same frame with the glare layer off, and compare the width of the halo as a fraction of the picture's width. It must come out the same. If it shrinks, your chain is sized from the output and not from the picture, and every halo measurement you have taken at a size other than the one you tuned at is wrong by that factor.
- **Do not look for the halo's width in the blur radius.** The kernel's reach is counted in texels of the level it blurs, so once the grid is fixed the halo's width follows from the chain's size far more than from the radius; and a kernel with a fixed, small number of taps cannot widen the halo past what the grid allows however large the radius is set. If your halo is too tight, make the chain coarser before you touch the radius.
- **This is a statement about the sizes a build is actually rendered at**, not a law for every resolution: the original was made for a small number of output modes and its grid is not identical in all of them. Fix your grid in the picture's own coordinates across the range of sizes you render and compare at, and leave it there.

**How the weight behaves above the knee (version 28).** The weight is zero up to the threshold and rises above it, as the bright-pass step above says, and two further things about it matter.

- **It is not normalised by how bright the pixel is.** The bright-pass emits the pixel's own colour multiplied by the weight, and the weight follows the excess above the knee alone; it is not divided by the pixel's brightness. So the light a source contributes grows with the excess **and** with the source's own brightness, and a source far above the knee outweighs one grazing it by much more than the difference in their excesses. This is what keeps the glare on the folds: the band's crests sit far above the knee while a background that passes at all only grazes it. A build that divides its weight by the pixel's brightest channel throws that away and shifts its glare from the folds towards the background - which is the wrong way round for this effect, and shows up as a broad lift with a weak halo on the band.
- **It is carried at the picture's own precision**, not in floating point. A source barely above the knee therefore contributes exactly nothing, rather than a trace. Over a large area that is the whole difference between no lift and a visible one, because a trace from every pixel of a broad region adds up to something a measure of the finished frame can see while nothing adds up to nothing. Where your own background sits close to its knee, this is worth more than the shape of the knee is.

**The mood sets the threshold; the picture never does (version 20).** The exposure, the curve and the threshold of the bright-pass are properties of the mood (section 7) and of nothing else. None of them is recomputed from the frame's own content: the threshold does not follow the background's brightness, the month's palette, a measured peak of the image, or anything else that changes from frame to frame. Raising the threshold each frame to sit above the background's own brightness, so that a bright palette can never bloom, is wrong in two ways. It makes the glare on the wave weaker in a bright month than in a dark one, which the original does not do; and if the margin is at all generous it takes the daytime glare off the wave as well, leaving the glare layer contributing nothing to any frame. The original needs no such guard and does not have one. **Version 24:** version 20 went on to say that in every mood and month the background by itself stays well below the threshold. That is right for most of the year and wrong for part of it, as the next paragraph says. Put the threshold where the mood puts it, and let the picture fall where it may.

**The background itself glares in a few weeks of the year (version 24; the weeks corrected and the size given in version 28).** The bright-pass follows the brightest single channel of a pixel, not how bright the pixel looks, so a month whose background is strongly coloured reaches the threshold while looking no brighter than a neutral month that stays clear of it. In the day mood, in the **last weeks before the turn of the year** - version 24 said the weeks around it; the window opens in the second half of November and is over well before New Year - the brightest part of the background by itself passes the threshold through the middle hours of the day, over a broad patch of the lower part of the screen, and the glare picks it out: a soft, wide, faint lift lying over the background itself, well away from the band, with no wave under it. It comes and goes over the weeks as the month colour changes, and it is gone by the dusk hold. The channel that carries it there is red.

**And it is very small (version 28).** This feature is at the very edge of what the output can show. At its strongest the background's own glare lifts a small patch of the frame by a few output levels, and over much of the window the weight, carried at the picture's own precision, lets nothing through at all - the background clears the threshold and still produces no glare. It is a real feature of the original rather than a fault to be guarded against, and a build that keeps its background clear of the threshold in every month will not have it; but a build whose background glare is a blob, or lifts a large part of the frame by many levels, is not reproducing this feature - it is doing something far larger, and the place to look is what its background is doing, not the glare. Elsewhere in the year the background does stay under the line, so only the wave's brightest folds glare and the background away from the band is the plain gradient.

**Check both sides of the threshold, month by month (version 24).** One threshold per mood has to serve the whole year, and the months you have footage of are not a fair test of it. For each month and each mood, measure on your own renders the brightest value your bright-pass sees with the wave drawn and with the wave left out, and compare both with that mood's threshold. What to expect: the wave's brightest folds pass it in most daylight months, with the margin varying a great deal from month to month; and the background by itself stays under it in most months, and in the weeks named above it does not. Where your folds fail to pass, the threshold is usually not what is at fault: either that month's background is too bright or the wave's folds are too dim, and that is where to look. One warning for the parts of this document that own the exposure, the tone curve and the palettes: treat the day threshold as lying inside the range the background's own brightest part covers over the year, so a change to the exposure, the curve or the palettes can move a month across the line in either direction, and the check above is worth repeating after one. **Version 27:** when both sides cannot hold at once in some month, say which side fails and by how much, and prefer the reading that what is fed to the bright-pass is wrong over the reading that the threshold is: the original's own background has room to spare under the line in every month but the weeks named above, so a background that passes by itself in many months is too bright for its mood before it is anything else.

Exposure, white point, radii, ratio and strength are all set per mood (section 7); for which moods glare most, see the Look section above.

## 6. Sparkles

### Look

- A dense field of tiny glowing particles. They are densest along and just around the band, often in loose trails along it, and thin out with distance. **Version 21 (corrects "a sprinkling of them reaches far across the screen, including near the top and bottom"):** the field keeps close to the band. Its density falls away quickly on either side, so the top and the bottom of the picture are all but empty of sparkles, and no part of the field reads as a cloud, a spray or a haze standing apart from the band. The row where sparkles are densest follows the band itself, and sparkles far from the band are rare at every moment, not only on average. The count stays roughly constant once the effect has been running for a while (section 11). **Version 25:** there is no large population of live sparkles too faint to make out. So if your field is sparser than the footage while your pool is full, look to where your sparkles are rather than to how bright they are; see "Where the pool is" under Construction. Count the points both at the picture's own size and after a downscale, and match both: a downscale merges neighbours, so the gap between the two counts says how crowded the field is, and a build can match one while missing the other badly.
- **Life:** each sparkle travels steadily in the direction it was flung, a little irregularly, fades in quickly after it appears, lives for several seconds (lifetimes vary), then fades out.
- **Space:** sparkles live in the same 3D space as the wave and are seen through the same camera. Some are in front of the sheet and some behind it, and their on-screen size and position follow from their 3D position.
- **Look:** each sparkle is a small point of light, mostly neutral white-grey; a few show iridescent colour that changes as they turn (see "Look (version 12)" below). Some twinkle. Most are small and sharp; some, far from the focus distance, are larger, softer and dimmer, like out-of-focus points of light (bokeh).
- All randomness comes from a seeded pseudo-random generator of your own (see section 9).
- **Motion (version 12).** Each sparkle keeps the velocity it is flung off the wave with for its whole life. Nothing visibly slows, lifts or drops it: any gravity must be too weak to bend a path visibly over a sparkle's life. Sparkles move about as fast as the band's own lines rise and fall (4.6), so they read as being thrown off the moving sheet, not floating. At any moment slightly more of them are moving up than down, and there is no visible downward settling. **Version 17:** there is no common pull in any direction, but each sparkle's path may curve gently its own way, and older sparkles' speeds spread more than young ones'. The slight upward majority comes from which sparkles stay in view, not from the births: as many are flung downwards as upwards, so do not bias the births upwards.
- **Time of day (version 12).** Their number and brightness are the same at every time of day. The mood changes the focus and size only slightly, never their brightness.
- **Look (version 12).** Mostly neutral white-grey: small hard-edged round dots, some seen as ovals or short dashes as they tumble, an occasional bright point with a soft halo, and large, flat, dim grey out-of-focus discs close to the camera. Only a few carry strong colour. No star rays. Among many faint sparkles a few stand out brightly.
- **Twinkle (version 21).** Sparkles do not shine steadily. A sparkle's brightness changes markedly from one frame to the next: it flares for a moment, dims again, and often drops out of sight altogether and comes back. Over its life it spends most of its time faint and reaches its brightest only in short flashes, so following one by eye across many frames is hard and one sparkle is easily taken for several. The twinkle comes from the lighting as the disc tumbles and from discs passing through edge-on (both under Construction), not from any random brightness of the sparkle itself. A field whose sparkles shine evenly from birth to death is wrong even when its count, its sizes and its motion are right.
- **Colour (version 21).** At every hour a clear minority of sparkles carries colour plainly enough to name, while the rest read as neutral. The tints seen are pinks and magentas, peach and yellow, red-orange, and some violet-blue; strong green or cyan is rare. Colour goes with brightness: the plainly tinted sparkles are found among the brighter ones, the many faint points read as neutral grey, and only at the very top of the range does a flaring core begin to wash back towards white. A single sparkle's tint changes as it turns. A field in which every sparkle carries the same faint tint, or in which the tinted ones are no brighter than the rest, is wrong. Judge colour on lossless footage: compressed recordings lose most of it.
- **Shape (version 15).** Every sparkle is a small, flat, round disc in 3D, not a camera-facing sprite. It tumbles with the sparkle's spin: seen face-on it is round, tilted it becomes an oval, and nearly edge-on a short thin line that is also fading out (version 16: a disc's opacity falls as it turns edge-on, so a fully edge-on disc is invisible), so some sparkles read as short dashes that change shape as they turn. Nothing stretches a sparkle along its direction of motion. Sparkles close to the camera turn towards it and show as round discs. **Version 21 (corrects version 17's "every disc is also turned a modest part of the way towards the camera at any distance, so no disc is ever seen fully edge-on"):** how far a disc is turned towards the camera is not the same for all sparkles. The turn grows with how near the sparkle is and with how large its disc appears on screen, and falls away to nothing with distance, so the great majority of sparkles, which are far off, keep their own tumbling orientation untouched. Those do pass through edge-on: such a disc thins to a faint line, goes out of sight for a while and opens up again. Only the few near, large discs are pulled round to face the viewer. A build that keeps even a modest turn at every distance has no edge-on population at all, because a turn that applies everywhere sets a floor on how far from face-on any disc can get; it then shows too few thin, faint and dash-like sparkles, too few that are on the edge of being seen at all, and too even a spread of brightness across the field. These brief edge-on passes are a large part of the twinkle described above. In focus, a disc has a crisp edge and a flat, even interior; out of focus, its edge softens. The disc fades to nothing well inside the patch it is drawn on, so no square or rectangular outline ever shows. The glint pass is round too.
- **Even sizes across the picture (version 25).** Apart from the out-of-focus few below, the points are all much the same size wherever they sit. The size of a point hardly depends on where in the picture it is: there is no gradient of size or of softness from one edge or one corner towards another, and no part of the field reads as dustier, softer or coarser than the rest. A point keeps its size as it travels, too; nothing swells as it drifts. So within one frame the spread of point sizes is narrow, and points much wider than the usual one are rare rather than merely uncommon. A field whose points grow towards one edge of the picture, or a noticeable part of which is softened, is wrong even when the usual size is right. What decides this is not the way a size is worked out — a build and the original both set a disc's size in the scene and let distance shrink it — but how wide a range of distances from the camera the field occupies compared with that distance itself, and where the focus range (under Construction) sits relative to that range of distances. Both of those are settings, and both are left to footage (below).
- **The out-of-focus few (version 25; corrects version 12's "large, flat, dim grey out-of-focus discs close to the camera").** The blurred ones are the farthest sparkles, not the nearest. At some hours a few sparkles in each frame are not points at all but small round discs: plainly wider than a sharp point, with a soft rim and an almost even interior, and dim — no brighter than the ordinary points around them and often fainter, though a few are much brighter. They drift among the sharp points, and they are what keeps the field from reading as a spray of identical dots. How many of them a frame holds changes markedly over the day: at some hours they can be found in any frame, at others the field is uniformly sharp and they are all but absent. This is one of the few places where the mood changes the sparkles plainly. A build whose far focus distance lies beyond everything in its scene shows the uniformly sharp field at every hour and never shows these discs at all. Wide and bright together is the wrong reading of it: if a build's widest soft patches are also among its brightest, what it is showing is not this population but an overgrown halo (below).
- **Halos (version 25).** A bright point that carries a halo keeps a sharp core; the halo does not swallow it into a fuzzy ball. The halo's light is gathered close in and then thins into a faint skirt that can still be told from the background well out from the core, so the whole thing reads as a crisp point sitting in a wide, very faint glow. A halo that is strong close in and gone a little further out is too compact, however its strength is set, and one that has spread the core's own light outwards to widen itself is wrong too, because the core stays sharp. What has to match is the whole profile from the core outwards, not the brightness at any one distance from it.
- **Irregularity of a path (version 25).** The irregularity of a sparkle's path reads mostly as a shimmer about a straight average course, with a bend one way that is real but builds up only slowly over a long life. Two things a build might do instead are both plainly wrong: paths that glide smoothly along a curve bent one way, and a field in which a few sparkles run away fast and are flung clear of the band. The original shows neither.

### Construction (how the original does it; do the same with your own values)

**Pool.** A fixed-size pool of particles. A free list holds the unused slots. Once the effect has been running for a while the pool is nearly always full, so births are limited by deaths. A birth with no free slot is simply dropped.

**Where the pool is (version 25).** With the pool full, what fills the frame is the part of the pool that is inside the picture. A sparkle outside the picture still holds its slot until it dies, so it costs a birth and shows nothing. Two things put live sparkles where they cannot be seen. First, where sparkles are born: they are born on the sheet, so births on parts of the sheet that lie outside the picture go straight into that unseen population. Second, how far outside the picture the faces of the bounding region sit: a sparkle that has left the picture but is still inside the region keeps its slot until its life ends. So if your field is sparser than the footage while your pool is full, measure what share of your own live pool is inside the picture (below) before enlarging the pool: enlarging it leaves those unseen sparkles in place.

**Birth: where.** Sparkles are born **on the wave surface**, at sample points of the sheet's mesh. There are two sources:

- **Bursts (version 16):** on a large share of the steps, a batch of a fixed size, considerably more than a handful, each sparkle at an independently chosen random point of the sheet.
- **A walker (version 16):** a single walker. From time to time it starts at a random point of the sheet. It then emits one sparkle every other step while it steps point by point along the length. It stops after a random number of steps or at the sheet's end. This produces **trails** of sparkles along the sheet.
- **Balance (version 16).** Bursts supply nearly all births; trails are a small minority. Once the pool is full, the walker's births are the ones that are dropped, because its requests are queued after the burst's. A build whose sparkles are mostly trails is wrong.
- **Where on the sheet (version 17).** The random points are chosen uniformly in the surface's two parameters, not uniformly over the sheet's area, so births crowd where the sheet is compressed or bunched.

**Birth: velocity.**

- The base direction is the **wave point's own motion over the last step**: the sparkle is flung off the moving sheet. The direction is spread randomly within a narrow cone around that motion. (Version 17: the cone has a hard edge, and the deviation angle is spread evenly up to it; a spread with long tails, such as a Gaussian, is not the same.)
- A small fraction of births have the direction **reversed**.
- The component along the camera's viewing axis is removed, so new sparkles start moving parallel to the screen. The remainder is not rescaled, so a birth whose motion pointed partly towards or away from the camera starts slower (version 17).
- The speed grows with the wave point's speed, but more slowly than it (sublinearly), with a minimum and some random variation. A wave point that is not moving emits nothing.
- The sparkle starts at the point's current position.

**Life.**

- Each sparkle gets its own ageing rate, a random spread around a mean with a floor, so lifetimes vary.
- Its opacity ramps up over a small fraction of its life, holds at full, and ramps down over a slightly larger fraction at the end. (Version 17: both ramps are short and linear, a small part of the life; the fade-in is the quicker.)
- It dies when its life is over **or when it leaves a bounding region**: a large box around the scene, more generous in some directions than in others. (Version 17: the box's faces all lie outside the view, so its fade is never seen on screen.)

**Motion, per fixed step.**

- Acceleration = **jitter** + **gravity** (downwards, and so weak that it never visibly bends a sparkle's path; see "Motion (version 12)") − **friction** proportional to velocity (weak: it slows a sparkle only slightly over its life).
- **Jitter (version 25; corrects version 17's "the jitter is not fresh noise each step", "stays the same for long stretches" and "A push redrawn every step averages out to nothing visible").** The push, one value per axis, is mostly redrawn each step, but a weak part of it does not change over a sparkle's life, so a sparkle's average push is not quite zero. How strong the push is and how large that unchanging part is are two separate settings, both left to footage (below). Neither extreme is right. Fresh noise alone gives shimmer about straight average paths, no bend building up one way, and no growth of the spread of speeds with age. A push drawn once at birth and used unchanged, with no fresh noise at all, gives smoothly bent paths, a long tail of sparkles that run away fast, and sparkles flung clear of the band, none of which the original shows. The original is mostly shimmer, with a one-way bend that is real but a minority of a long path's wander, and a spread of speeds that grows mildly with age. The part that does not change does not decay over a sparkle's life either, so the bend has no timescale of its own: it simply accumulates. The two settings pull the same way on a path's curvature, so a weak push with a larger unchanging part and a stronger push with a smaller one can look much alike, and fitting either setting alone moves the curvature the wrong way.
- Velocity is updated from the acceleration, then position from velocity (semi-implicit Euler). The step rate is in section 11.
- **Spin:** each sparkle carries an orientation (a quaternion). Every step it rotates by a small angular velocity that varies slowly and smoothly with the sparkle's age (different slow oscillations on each axis), and it is renormalised. (Version 17: the oscillations are the same function of the life fraction for every sparkle, a fixed schedule over a sparkle's life, not per-sparkle random frequencies or phases.)

**Rendering.**

- Each sparkle is a flat patch at its 3D position, turned by the sparkle's spin (turned towards the camera when close to it, and, version 21, only in so far as it is near and looks large on screen, as under Shape above) and carrying a round disc (version 15), drawn with additive blending and with its opacity from the life ramp.
- **Size (version 17).** A disc's size is set in the scene, like the wave's, so it looks smaller the farther away it is. There is no random size per sparkle. The disc sits well inside its patch.
- **Lighting and colour (version 16; replaces "iridescent tint by orientation").** The sparkle's facing direction comes from its quaternion. Each disc is lit by **one fixed light at a point in the scene**, not by a set of directions: a two-sided diffuse term from the facing direction, plus a **sharp specular highlight** from the half-way vector between the directions to the light and to the camera, the highlight tinted by the iridescent colour. The sum passes through a **soft saturating curve** (an exponential approach to white), so the brightest sparkles saturate instead of blowing out. The iridescent colour comes from a **two-dimensional colour map** indexed by the two screen-plane components of the facing direction (a "lit sphere" lookup), raised to an exponent. There is **no per-sparkle random brightness**: the spread from faint to bright, and the twinkle, come from the lighting as the discs spin (a disc flares when its orientation catches the highlight) and from the fades below. **Version 17:** the light is in front of the camera, inside the field of view, so discs flare at moderate tilts and not only when nearly face-on. In the settings used the exponent leaves the colour map unchanged. **Version 21 (corrects version 17's "the map keeps roughly the same moderate saturation everywhere"):** the map's saturation varies strongly from place to place across it, with broad near-neutral, almost white regions lying between strongly coloured ones. Its hues are not spread evenly either: the tints that appear on screen (see Look) are not an even walk round a hue wheel, so a map built as one is wrong. How bright and how strongly coloured each part of the map is is not fixed here: build the map so that the field on screen matches the footage in the distribution of per-sparkle colour saturation and in how colour goes with brightness. The map's colour is carried by the highlight at full strength, not mixed towards white. A smooth wheel of one saturation cannot stand in for such a map whatever that one saturation is set to: a pale wheel leaves the whole field neutral, a stronger one tints everything mildly without ever producing the strongly coloured few, and either reads too blue and cyan. Most sparkles still read as neutral because the map tints only the highlight, because much of the map is near-neutral, and because the saturating curve washes the very strongest flares back towards white.
- **Tilt fade (version 16).** A disc's opacity falls smoothly as it turns edge-on (a power curve of one minus the cosine between its facing direction and the direction to the camera), so a fully edge-on disc is invisible; the dashes of version 15 are discs at moderate tilts. Opacity also fades out smoothly as a sparkle approaches the faces of the bounding region, and the depth-of-field darkening below reduces it further. **Version 25:** how steeply that fade bites as a disc turns is a setting, not a given, and it governs two things at once — how many sparkles a frame shows as separate points, and how much of the field sits at the faint end of the brightness range. A fade steep enough that a disc is hard to make out unless it is nearly face-on empties the faint end and takes a large part of the population off the screen while leaving the bright end looking right, so a build can be far too sparse and still match the brightest sparkles. Fit it from the count of points and from the whole distribution of peak levels together, never from the bright end alone (below), and keep the edge-on passes: a build that holds the count up by refusing to let discs fade loses the twinkle instead.
- **Depth of field:** each sparkle's size and brightness depend on its distance from a **focus range** (a near and a far focus distance, each with a soft transition). In focus: small and sharp. **Version 17 (corrects "dimmer, so the total light stays similar"):** out of focus, a disc grows modestly and its edge softens until it fills its patch, but its brightness per pixel drops only a little (more the closer it is). The original does not dim blurred discs to keep their total light constant, does not grey them and has no separate fade close to the camera, so a blurred disc near the camera carries more light than a sharp one. In the settings used only the near side of the focus range is reached: no sparkle is as far away as the far focus distance. **Version 25 (corrects that last sentence).** It is the other way round. Very little of the field ever comes near enough to the camera for the near side to blur it appreciably, while a minority of the field is farther off than the far focus distance and is blurred by the far side — and the far side is where the out-of-focus discs of the Look section come from. The far transition is never carried to completion: even the farthest sparkle is only part of the way along it, so a blurred disc is always a partly blurred one. The far end sits inside the range of distances the field occupies, and near enough to the thick of it that a small change in the far end changes how much of the field is blurred a great deal — which is why the mood's small shift of it (below) shows plainly on screen, and why a build that puts its far focus beyond everything in its scene has no blurred population at all and no change of focus over the day. A build a noticeable part of whose field is softened by the **near** side has its focus range in the wrong place relative to its field.
- **Second glint pass:** the same sparkles are drawn again additively as a second, softer glint. Its strength follows the mood's glare setting. **Version 17:** the glint is a round, soft halo that faces the camera, and its size and strength follow the sparkle's specular highlight: a sparkle that is not catching the highlight has essentially no glint, and one that is gets a halo much larger than its disc. The glint carries only the highlight's (tinted) light, blurred sparkles get it too, and it is slightly weaker at night than in the other moods. **Version 25:** the halo's patch is wide while the light inside it is gathered near the middle, so a bright sparkle shows a narrow core and a faint skirt reaching well out from it (see "Halos" under Look). A compact bell — bright close in, gone a little further out — is the wrong profile however its strength is set, and widening the halo by spreading the core's own light outwards is wrong too. How wide the patch is and how fast the light falls away inside it are settings, left to footage (below). This pass is where a bright sparkle's skirt comes from and it adds a little to the peak of a bright core, but it creates no points of its own: a field that is too sparse is not short of halo.
- The mood (section 7) shifts the far focus distance and the sparkle size slightly. (Version 17: sparkles are slightly larger at night and dawn than by day and at dusk.)

**What to measure on footage (version 21).** Several of the settings above are pinned down better by the original's own footage than by judgment, and this document deliberately fixes none of them. Measure each on lossless footage, over several conditions and several seconds of each, and fit your own setting to what you measure:

- **How many separate points a frame holds, and how much of your pool is on screen (version 25).** Count the separate points in single frames of the footage at the footage's own size, and in your own renders of the same conditions at the same size; a downscaled frame merges neighbours and hides this. Count the faint points apart from the bright ones, since the two can be off in opposite directions. Do the count both ways, at the footage's own size and after a downscale, and match both: the gap between the two is how crowded the field is, and matching one proves nothing about the other. Then, for the same frames, count how many sparkles your own simulation holds alive and how many of those are inside the picture. A detector finds much the same number of points for each on-screen sparkle in any build, so those three numbers together say which fault you have: too small an on-screen population, or sparkles held and never shown. Fit the first by bringing the field onto the screen, not by enlarging the pool.
- **The size of a disc in focus.** Measure the width of small bright sparkles at a fixed level below their peak, the same rule applied to the footage and to your own renders, at full size: a downscaled frame hides this difference. Fit the size to the measured width. How many separate points a frame yields is not set by the disc's size on its own, so do not fit the size to a count.
- **The spread of point sizes within one frame, and where in the picture the wider ones sit (version 25).** Measure the width or the area of points in single frames at the footage's own size, and compare not only the usual value but the upper end of the spread, and both of those separately for different parts of the picture. Fit how wide a range of distances from the camera your field occupies, and where the focus range sits relative to it, so that the spread and its evenness across the picture both match. One trap is worth knowing: widening the range of distances a field occupies, inside a focus arrangement like the original's, does not read as a mild softening of the whole field — it puts occasional wide blobs wherever the field is farthest from the camera, which the eye catches at once.
- **How many out-of-focus discs a frame holds, hour by hour (version 25).** Count, per frame and at the footage's own size, the blobs much wider than a sharp point, and record their width, their peak level and how even their interior is. Do this at hours whose moods are far apart, and fit the far focus distance for each mood to the counts and the widths. Do not fit it to the evenness of a disc's interior: that follows from the disc being wide with a soft rim, and there are too few such discs in a frame for it to settle anything.
- **A sparkle's overall brightness.** Measure the upper part of the distribution of per-sparkle peak levels at the footage's own resolution, and in particular how close the brightest cores come to the top of the output range. **Version 25:** measure the whole distribution and not only its upper part — the share of points in every band from the faintest that can be told from the background up to the brightest — because a build can match the bright end while missing most of the faint end, and because the faint end and the count of points move together with how steeply a disc fades as it turns.
- **The shape of a bright core's halo (version 25).** Measure the light around bright cores at a series of distances out from the core, on footage and in your own renders at the footage's own size, and fit the halo's extent and its falloff to the whole profile. One distance is not enough: a halo can be too strong close in and too weak further out at the same time, which is what a patch too small on screen, or a falloff too tight inside it, looks like.
- **How strongly coloured the coloured minority is, and how the colour map's own brightness and strength of colour vary across it.** Measure the whole distribution of per-sparkle colour saturation, not only its average, the share of sparkles above a strong-colour level, and how colour goes with brightness, on lossless footage only; build the map so that the field on screen matches all three.
- **How fast a sparkle's look changes over its life.** Measure how much, and how quickly, the brightness along one sparkle's track varies, against how long that track lasts. This fixes the rate of the spin schedule relative to a lifetime.
- **The push against gravity, and how large its unchanging part is (version 25; replaces "The held jitter push against gravity").** Two observables for two settings. From long tracks, measure how far a track departs from a straight line and what share of that departure is a bend one way rather than wander to either side; and measure how much wider the spread of speeds is among old sparkles than among young ones. Straight paths with a common lean mean the push is too weak beside gravity; smooth one-way curves, a tail of fast sparkles and sparkles flung clear of the band mean the unchanging part is too large. Fit the strength and the unchanging part together, since neither observable can settle both alone. Do not try to measure a frame-to-frame change of velocity from footage: a detector reports positions to the nearest pixel, so what it gives from one frame to the next is its own rounding, and it comes out the same whatever the sparkles do.

## 7. Time of day, month and themes

- **Clock**: in normal use the effect reads the viewer's local date and time and updates continuously.
- **Moods**: the day has four moods: night, dawn, day and dusk. Each is a complete set of parameters for the wave (brightness, Fresnel-like strength and response, thread spacing, pre-filtering, lattice scales, envelope crossover, camera tilt), the composite's exposure and tone curve (4.5, version 17), the glare (threshold, radii, ratio, strength) and the sparkles (focus, size, glint). Each mood holds for a while around its part of the day and then blends into the next with a smooth ease-in-out fade lasting a few hours, longer than the hold (hours in section 11). The blend is linear in every parameter with one shared factor.
- **Background**: the month blend and the background's own day/night weight are described in section 3.
- **Themes**: besides the automatic mode (theme 0), there are twelve fixed colour themes (1 to 12) that do not follow the clock or the date. Their colour families are: 1 silver, 2 yellow, 3 light green, 4 pink, 5 green, 6 lavender, 7 teal, 8 blue, 9 purple, 10 gold, 11 brown, 12 red. **Theme n is month n's palette, frozen as it looks at midday at the start of month n, with the day mood for the wave, glare and sparkles, whatever the actual time.** So the theme colours and the month palettes are the same twelve palettes.

## 9. Deterministic render interface (required)

This interface is for your own side-by-side testing (section 10): it lets you render exactly the same frame again, at any size, for any time, theme and seed. It must be exact.

### URL parameters

`index.html?mode=score&time=<ISO local date-time>&theme=<0..12>&seed=<int>&frames=<n>&w=<px>&h=<px>&layers=<list>`

- `mode=score`: no clock, no animation loop, no UI. The page renders only on request. (The name is kept from version 2.)
- `time`: the local date and time the scene represents, for example `2026-10-15T12:00:00`.
- `theme`: 0 = automatic (palette from `time`), 1..12 = fixed themes.
- `seed`: seeds every random choice (physics disturbance, sparkle births). The same URL must always give the same image, bit for bit, on the same browser and GPU.
- `frames`: the simulation runs this many steps of exactly 1/60 s each (this interface's convention), from a seeded start, before the frame is rendered. The state at frame 0 must already look like a typical running state: pre-run (warm up) the wave and the sparkles internally so that, for example, the sparkle population is not empty at the start. Frames `n` and `n+1` must be consecutive steps, so that motion between them can be measured.
- `w`, `h`: the size of the rendered image in pixels.
- `layers`: a comma-separated subset of `bg,wave,glare,sparkles,dither` to draw; default all. Omitted layers contribute nothing (for example `layers=bg` renders the background only, with no dither).

### JavaScript

The page defines `window.XMBW` with:

- `ready`: a Promise that resolves once the page can render (shaders compiled, textures made).
- `render(opts)`: renders one frame and returns `{ width, height, data }`, where `data` is a `Uint8ClampedArray` of RGBA bytes, rows top to bottom, alpha 255. `opts` takes the same fields as the URL (`time, theme, seed, frames, w, h, layers`); any field it omits defaults to the URL's value. Calling it with a larger `frames` than the previous call may continue the same simulation instead of restarting, as long as the result equals a fresh render with those settings.

## 10. Testing against public references

- Compare your renders **side by side with genuine public footage and screenshots of the original**. A vetted list of public references is supplied separately. Use only references from that list or ones the user gives you directly. Fan recreations, re-renders and recoloured versions do not count.
- Use section 9 to render the matching moment: the month and time of day shown in the reference, the matching theme, the same picture size. Where the footage shows motion, compare motion over the same span of seconds.
- Judge the **overall look first**: background colours and gradient, the wave's placement, size, brightness, translucency and folds, the density and look of the sparkles, the glare, and the pace of the motion. Then refine details.
- **Ignore other on-screen elements** in the footage (menus, icons, clock, text, cursors) and compression artefacts. Your output never contains them.
- Your wave and sparkles move independently of any reference, so do not aim for pixel equality. Aim for a frame that would be indistinguishable in kind.
- **Log every reference you use** in `docs/REFERENCES.md`: the URL or source, the timestamp or frame, what it shows (month, time, theme if known) and what you compared or changed because of it.
